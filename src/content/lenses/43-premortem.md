## Principle

Before a plan is locked in, state that it has already failed and ask each person to
write down why.

## Explanation

Inversion is the general move: think about failure to improve the chance of success. The
premortem, described by the psychologist Gary Klein, is a specific group procedure built
on it, and its value comes from two details that are easy to drop.

The first is the framing. "What could go wrong?" invites hedged speculation. "It is a
year from now and this has failed — what happened?" asks for an explanation of a fact.
Research on "prospective hindsight" found that imagining an outcome has already occurred
helps people generate more, and more specific, reasons for it. The effect is modest, but
it costs nothing.

The second is social. Once a plan has momentum, raising a doubt looks like opposing the
leader or the team. A premortem makes producing failure stories everyone's assignment,
so the person with the uncomfortable concern is doing the task rather than breaking
ranks. For the same reason, people should write their reasons independently before
anyone speaks; otherwise the first, most senior story shapes all the others.

The output is a short list, not a mood: the assumptions that would hurt most if wrong,
the early signal for each, and who will watch for it.

## Military Example

Before a field exercise, the planning team writes the failure report in advance:
resupply arrives late, communications fail in a low-lying area, and a relief in place
leaves a boundary uncovered. Each item is checked against the plan. A spare
communications path and a changed relief timing cost little before deployment and a
great deal after it.

## Leadership Example

Before a restructure, a leadership team imagines that six months later key staff have
left and customers heard about the change through rumour. Written individually, the
stories converge on gaps nobody had raised in discussion: managers had no script for
their teams' questions, and the announcement sequence put customers last. Both became
owned actions with dates.

## Product Example

A team imagines a new integration launched to little adoption and expensive support. The
failure stories point to unverified demand, a partner API that changes without notice,
and no way for support to diagnose faults. The team interviews ten target customers,
adds versioning, and sets a support-readiness check before committing to the full build.

## Questions

- It is a year from now and this has failed. What happened?
- What has someone here privately worried about but not said?
- Which assumption, if wrong, would hurt us most?
- What early signal would tell us the failure story has started?

## Common Misuse

A premortem held after the decision is made is theatre: the group lists risks it has no
intention of acting on and feels thorough. Run constantly, it becomes a ritual of
pessimism and a polite veto on anything ambitious. It also finds only the failures
people can imagine; the surprises that matter most are often the ones nobody in the room
could have named. Use it once, at the point where the plan can still change, then
commit.
