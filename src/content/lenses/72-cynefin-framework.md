## Principle

Match the method to the kind of problem: follow practice where cause and effect is
obvious, use expertise where it can be analysed, experiment where it can only be
understood afterwards, and act first where there is none.

## Explanation

Dave Snowden developed the Cynefin framework (a Welsh word, roughly "habitat") to help
people recognise what kind of situation they are in before choosing how to act. It
distinguishes four main domains.

In clear situations, cause and effect is obvious and best practice exists: recognise the
case and apply the procedure. In complicated situations, cause and effect can be worked
out, but only with expertise and analysis; there may be several good answers. In complex
situations, cause and effect becomes clear only in hindsight, because the parts interact
and adapt — markets, organisations, people. Analysis cannot predict them, so you run
small, safe-to-fail probes, watch what happens, and amplify what works. In chaotic
situations there is no discernible cause and effect, and the first need is to act to
establish some stability. A fifth state, confusion, is not knowing which domain you are
in.

The framework's value is in exposing a category error. Treating a complex problem as
complicated produces confident plans that reality ignores. Treating a complicated
problem as complex produces experiments where an expert could simply have given the
answer. Treating a crisis as a planning exercise wastes the moments that matter.

Cynefin is a sense-making framework built from practice, not an empirically validated
classification, and it is often simplified into a two-by-two grid that its author
rejects. Its usefulness is as a prompt, not a diagnosis.

## Leadership Example

A leader faces three problems in a week. A payroll error has an established correction
procedure and is fixed by following it. A pension-scheme change is complicated, so the
leader commissions advice from specialists. A new cross-team way of working is complex:
nobody can predict how people will respond, so the leader trials it in one area with a
review date, rather than designing it fully in advance.

## Product Example

A team is debating the cause of falling engagement and has commissioned a large
analysis. It recognises that the behaviour depends on how users, content and
recommendations interact, and that the same change has produced different results in
different months. Instead of a predictive model, it runs several small, reversible
experiments in parallel and keeps the ones that work. When an outage hits, it switches
to stabilising first and investigating afterwards.

## Questions

- Is cause and effect here obvious, analysable, visible only afterwards, or absent?
- Are we treating something complex as if analysis could predict it?
- Are we experimenting where an expert could just tell us?
- What would tell us the situation has moved into a different domain?

## Common Misuse

Labelling a problem "complex" can become an excuse to avoid analysis or to dismiss
expertise that exists. The domains are also not fixed properties: parts of the same
problem can sit in different domains, and situations move between them. Arguing about
which box a problem belongs in is a poor use of time. Use the framework to choose the
next step, then revise as evidence arrives.
