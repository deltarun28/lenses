## Principle

People who are responsible for a course of action tend to commit more to it as it fails,
because stopping would mean admitting the original decision was wrong.

## Explanation

The organisational psychologist Barry Staw demonstrated the effect in the 1970s: people
who had personally made an investment decision that went badly allocated more additional
money to it than people who inherited the same situation from someone else. Later
research has broadly supported the pattern, while showing that its strength varies with
how public the decision was, how responsible the person feels, and how close the project
appears to completion.

Several forces combine. Sunk costs feel like a reason to continue, though only future
costs and benefits should matter. Public commitment makes reversal look like
inconsistency, and inconsistency is socially costly. And the person best placed to judge
the evidence is the one with the most to lose from acknowledging it.

Consistency is normally valuable. It makes people predictable and their commitments
credible. The trap is when protecting the appearance of consistency becomes more
important than responding to what is now known.

Because the pressure is personal, the reliable remedies are structural. Agree before
starting what evidence would trigger a review, so stopping becomes following the plan
rather than abandoning it. Separate the decision to continue from the person who made
the decision to begin. And make "we learned this does not work" an acceptable outcome
rather than a confession.

## Military Example

An operation to take a position stalls with heavy losses. The commander who planned it
requests another attempt with more troops, arguing that the ground already gained cannot
be given up. A higher commander, who did not plan the attack, asks the question that
matters: if we were starting now, knowing what we know, would we attack here? The answer
is no, and the effort is redirected.

## Leadership Example

A manager has championed a system replacement for two years. New estimates double the
remaining cost, and the benefits case has weakened. Stopping would embarrass the manager
publicly. A review point agreed at the outset, run by someone outside the project, asks
whether the original assumptions still hold. The project is cut back to its working
components without the decision becoming a personal contest.

## Product Example

A team announced a major feature at a customer conference. Early usage shows customers
need something different. The pressure is to ship the announced version anyway. The team
explains what it learned and ships the smaller feature customers actually use. It loses
less trust by changing course openly than it would have by delivering a promise it no
longer believed in.

## Questions

- If I were starting fresh today, knowing what I know, would I begin this?
- Which costs am I counting that are already spent?
- Who made the original decision, and are they the right person to review it?
- What evidence did we agree, in advance, would make us stop?

## Common Misuse

Persistence through difficulty is often exactly right; many worthwhile projects look
like failures midway, and abandoning everything at the first setback is its own error.
Labelling every continued commitment as escalation makes long efforts impossible.
Reputation and credibility are also legitimate costs, not irrational ones. The question
is not whether you are committed, but whether you would still make the commitment
knowing what you now know.
