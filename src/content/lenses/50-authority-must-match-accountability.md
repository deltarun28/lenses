## Principle

The power to decide and the duty to answer for the result should sit with the same
person.

## Explanation

Explicit Accountability asks whether anyone owns an outcome. This lens asks whether the
owner can actually affect it, and whether the people who can affect it have to answer
for it. The idea is old: Henri Fayol, one of the first writers on management, listed in
1916 that authority and responsibility should correspond.

Mismatches come in two forms, and they fail differently.

Accountability without authority makes someone answer for an outcome while the decisions
that determine it — budget, staffing, priorities, approvals — are made elsewhere. The
result is blame without leverage. People in that position protect themselves with paper
trails, escalate everything, or burn out, and capable people learn to avoid such roles.

Authority without accountability lets someone decide without bearing the consequences.
Economists call the general pattern moral hazard: people take more risk when someone
else pays for it. Decisions drift towards the decider's convenience, and costs are
pushed onto whoever has to live with them.

Mismatches rarely arrive by design. They accumulate: a central approval added here, a
shared service created there, a decision moved to a senior forum that does not then own
the outcome. Each change is sensible on its own, and the result is roles whose
responsibilities no longer match their powers. A simple diagnosis is to list the
decisions that determine an outcome and write down who makes each one.

## Military Example

A company commander is responsible for a unit's live-firing qualification, but range
bookings, ammunition and transport are allocated by separate headquarters cells with
their own priorities. The commander can answer for the result but not change it. The fix
is either to give the commander an allocation to manage, or to make the cells share
accountability for the qualification rate.

## Leadership Example

A product manager is held accountable for revenue but cannot set prices, influence the
sales team or change what engineering builds. The accountability is narrowed to what the
role controls — adoption and retention of the product's features — and the manager is
given a formal voice in pricing decisions. Both changes make it possible for the manager
to succeed or fail on their own decisions.

## Product Example

The engineer on call is accountable for keeping a service running, but cannot roll back
another team's change without that team's approval, which takes an hour to obtain at
night. The on-call engineer is given authority to roll back any change affecting the
service, with an obligation to inform the owning team immediately. Outages shorten.

## Questions

- What decisions determine this outcome, and who makes each one?
- Am I holding someone to account for decisions they cannot make?
- Who decides here without bearing the consequences?
- When we last centralised a decision, did the accountability move with it?

## Common Misuse

Perfect alignment is impossible. Almost every outcome depends on things outside anyone's
control, and demanding full authority before accepting accountability becomes a way of
never owning anything; Extreme Ownership is the counterweight. Influence counts, not
only formal power. And some separation of authority is deliberate: audit, segregation of
duties and checks and balances exist to stop the person with power from also being the
only judge of its use. Look for the mismatches nobody intended.
