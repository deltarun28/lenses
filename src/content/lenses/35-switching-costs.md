## Principle

Retention is often not satisfaction. It is the cost of leaving.

## Explanation

Switching costs come in several forms, and the expensive ones are rarely financial:
retraining, data migration, integration rework, contractual terms, and the risk that the
alternative is worse. Together they set how much dissatisfaction a customer will absorb
before acting.

This cuts both ways and both are worth seeing clearly. As a supplier, switching costs
are why a mediocre incumbent keeps its customers — and why measuring retention as
evidence of satisfaction is a mistake that ends abruptly when a migration path appears.
As a buyer, they are the thing to examine before you commit, because they determine
your position at every subsequent renewal.

The strategic version: your leverage in any ongoing relationship is set at the moment
you enter it, by how expensive you allowed leaving to become.

## Military Example

Selecting a platform commits far more than the purchase: training pipelines,
maintenance facilities, spares holdings, doctrine and skilled personnel all accumulate
around it. Twenty years later the cost of changing supplier includes all of that, which
is why procurement decisions are effectively decades long and why the negotiating
position at each upgrade is weaker than at the original competition.

## Leadership Example

An organisation on a core system that no longer fits stays because migration would take
two years and consume the change capacity for everything else. The system is not
retained because it is good. Recognising this changes the conversation from whether to
switch to how to reduce the exit cost incrementally, so the option exists later.

## Product Example

A company with high retention concludes customers love the product. The accurate reading
may be that exports are difficult, integrations are bespoke and staff are trained on it.
The distinction shows up the moment a competitor offers automated migration, at which
point churn arrives all at once and looks sudden — though the dissatisfaction had been
there for years.

## Questions

- Are they staying because it is good, or because leaving is expensive?
- What would it actually take for us to leave, in time and risk rather than money?
- Am I accumulating a dependency now that will price my next negotiation?
- If someone removed the exit cost tomorrow, how many would go?

## Common Misuse

Deliberately raising switching costs — obstructing exports, complicating migration — is
a strategy that works and reliably breeds resentment, and it is increasingly constrained
by regulation. It also substitutes for improvement: an organisation defended by lock-in
stops learning what customers want, because the feedback that would tell it has been
suppressed. And high switching costs make acquisition harder in the same measure, since
the customers you want are locked into someone else.
