## Principle

When part of a skill becomes cheap, its value moves to the parts that remain scarce.

## Explanation

Expertise is a bundle: knowing facts, carrying out procedures, judging situations,
dealing with people, taking responsibility for the result. New technologies rarely
replace the whole bundle. They make one component cheap. A basic economic principle then
applies: when something becomes cheaper, the things used alongside it — its complements
— become more valuable.

The economist James Bessen documented an example. Cash machines spread through American
banks from the 1970s, and bank tellers were widely expected to disappear. For decades
they did not: cheaper branches meant banks opened more of them, and the teller's job
shifted from counting cash towards dealing with customers and selling services. Teller
numbers later declined as online banking spread, so the example shows both halves of the
pattern: the value moved, and it did not stay still.

The value tends to move towards judgement, towards combining many inputs into a
decision, towards handling exceptions, towards taking responsibility, and towards
checking whatever is now cheap to produce. Experts who define themselves by the
component that has become cheap lose ground; those who move towards its complements
gain. And there is a less visible effect: if newcomers used to learn the scarce parts by
doing the now-cheap tasks, the route into the profession can narrow just as the scarce
parts become more valuable.

The question is durable because the pattern recurs: printing and scribes, calculators
and arithmetic, spreadsheets and bookkeeping, search engines and recall, and now
artificial intelligence and many kinds of drafting.

## Military Example

Satellite navigation made position-finding cheap and almost effortless. Plotting routine
positions on a map lost most of its value; interpreting terrain, judging routes and
navigating when satellite signals are jammed gained value. Forces that let map-reading
skills lapse found that the cheap capability had made the old skill rare exactly where
it mattered most.

## Leadership Example

Legal databases made case research fast. In a law firm, the value of senior lawyers
shifted further towards advising clients on what to do, negotiating and managing
relationships. Junior lawyers' traditional training work — days spent finding cases —
shrank, and the firm had to design new ways for them to develop the judgement that the
research work used to build.

## Product Example

Machine translation produces drafts in seconds. A translation team's value moves to
domain review, cultural adaptation, terminology control and responsibility for errors in
high-stakes documents. The team reorganises: low-risk content is published from machine
drafts with spot checks, and its translators concentrate on the material where a mistake
is costly.

## Questions

- Which part of this expertise is becoming cheap?
- What becomes scarcer and more valuable as a result?
- How did people previously learn the scarce part, and does that route still exist?
- Am I defending a component of my work, or moving towards its complements?

## Common Misuse

Forecasts of which skills will hold their value have a poor record, and confident
predictions are often wrong in detail. The shift is also not painless: individuals bear
real costs, and "move to the higher-value work" is easy advice to give and hard to
follow. Some skills that become cheap in normal conditions remain essential in
exceptional ones. Watch what is actually becoming scarce, rather than assuming you know.
