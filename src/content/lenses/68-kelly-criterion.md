## Principle

Even when an opportunity is favourable, how much you commit should depend on how large
your advantage is and how sure you are of it.

## Explanation

Expected value tells you whether a bet is worth taking. It does not tell you how much to
stake. A favourable bet with too large a stake can still ruin you, because a run of
losses — which favourable bets also produce — can end the game before the advantage pays
off.

In 1956 the scientist John Kelly worked out the stake that maximises long-run growth in
repeated bets with a known edge. Its general shape is intuitive: commit more when the
advantage is larger and the odds are better; commit less when they are not; commit
nothing when there is no edge. Betting more than the Kelly amount reduces growth and
increases the chance of ruin; betting much more is reliably disastrous.

Outside a casino, the formula's assumptions rarely hold. You seldom know the true
probabilities, the bets are not identical, and an outcome may be a one-off.
Overestimating your edge — the usual error — leads directly to over-commitment. For that
reason practitioners who use it, in investing and elsewhere, commonly stake only a
fraction of the Kelly amount.

The durable lesson is not the formula but the separation: first, is this a good bet?
Second, given how uncertain my estimate is, how much should I put on it? The second
answer is usually smaller than enthusiasm suggests.

## Leadership Example

A leader sees promising early demand for a new service, but from four customers in one
sector. The opportunity looks favourable; the estimate is very uncertain. Rather than
staff a full division, the leader funds a small team with a defined budget. As
independent evidence accumulates from other sectors, the commitment grows. The size of
the bet tracks the strength of the evidence, not the excitement.

## Product Example

An experiment shows that a new onboarding message improves activation in one customer
segment. The team is confident the effect is real but unsure of its size elsewhere. It
rolls the change out to a quarter of users, measures, then increases the share. A full
immediate rollout would have staked the whole user base on an estimate drawn from one
segment.

## Questions

- Is there a real edge here, and how do I know?
- How uncertain is my estimate of it?
- What happens to me after several losses in a row?
- Am I sizing this to the evidence, or to my enthusiasm?

## Common Misuse

Applying a precise formula to vague beliefs produces false precision: a calculation
built on guessed probabilities looks rigorous and is not. Even with good data, full
Kelly sizing produces swings most people and organisations cannot tolerate. Many
important decisions are unique rather than repeated, so the long-run logic does not
apply. And it ignores what cannot be expressed in money, such as reputation and safety.
Use the idea for calibration and survival, not as a mathematical costume for a hunch.
