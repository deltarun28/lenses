## Principle

Beliefs are estimates with a starting point. New evidence adjusts them; it rarely
replaces them.

## Explanation

Two failures are common and opposite. The first is ignoring the starting point: a
striking piece of evidence produces a confident conclusion with no regard for how rare
the thing was to begin with. The second is refusing to move at all, where evidence
accumulates and the belief does not.

Bayesian thinking is the middle path, and it needs only two questions in practice. How
common is this normally? And how much more likely is the evidence I just saw if my
hypothesis is true than if it is false? Weak evidence — the kind that would appear
either way — should barely move you. Strong evidence should move you a long way.

You do not need the arithmetic to get the benefit. Holding "how common is this
normally" in mind before reacting to a single striking data point is most of the value.

## Military Example

A source reports an imminent attack on a specific installation. The report is treated
first against the base rate: how often do reports from this source, of this type, prove
accurate? If the answer is one in twenty, the report shifts posture rather than
triggering the full response. A second, independent indicator — unusual movement
observed by different means — is worth far more than a louder version of the first
report, because it is evidence that would be unlikely if the hypothesis were false.

## Leadership Example

A manager receives one complaint about a team lead and must decide what it means. The
useful question is not whether the complaint sounds serious. It is how often a complaint
of this kind arises about someone whose team has stable retention and consistent
delivery, versus how often it arises about someone whose team does not. One complaint
warrants a careful look; treating it as proof, or dismissing it outright, both discard
information.

## Product Example

A customer interview produces a compelling account of an unmet need, and the team wants
to build for it. The check is the base rate: of the last ten equally compelling
interviews, how many represented demand that showed up in behaviour? If the answer is
two, the interview justifies a cheap test, not a roadmap commitment. Three unprompted
reports from unconnected customers is a much stronger signal than one vivid story.

## Questions

- How common is this situation before I take the new evidence into account?
- Would I have seen this same evidence if my hypothesis were false?
- Is this genuinely new information, or a louder version of what I already had?
- What would change my mind, and how much of it would I need?

## Common Misuse

The language gets used as decoration — assigning a percentage to a hunch does not make
it calibrated, and false precision is worse than admitting uncertainty. The other
failure is treating base rates as destiny in situations that have genuinely changed;
history is the starting point, not a constraint. And updating on every piece of weak
evidence produces thrashing rather than learning.
