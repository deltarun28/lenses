## Principle

Information is worth gathering only if it could change what you do, and only up to the
point where the change would be worth more than the cost and delay of finding out.

## Explanation

Certainty feels valuable even when it is useless. More analysis raises confidence, and
confidence is pleasant, but a report that could not alter the decision has no decision
value however accurate it is.

Decision analysts make this precise. The value of a piece of information depends on
three things: how likely it is to change the choice, how much better the changed choice
would be, and what obtaining it costs in money and time. There is a useful ceiling, too:
no research can be worth more than the value of knowing the answer for certain. If even
perfect knowledge would not justify a month's delay, no study will.

Two practical rules follow. Name the decision and the result that would reverse it
before commissioning any research; if no plausible result would change the choice, stop.
And look for the single uncertainty that drives the decision rather than refining what
is already known. Practitioners in measurement, notably Douglas Hubbard, observe that
organisations often measure most precisely the variables that matter least, because
those are the easiest to measure. That is an observation from consulting experience
rather than a controlled finding, but it is a common pattern.

## Leadership Example

A business considering expansion into a new region begins a large market study covering
size, competitors and pricing. Asked what result would stop the expansion, the team
realises only one does: whether local buyers will accept a long delivery lead time. A
three-month sales pilot answers that directly. The study is cut to the single question
that matters, and the decision is made a quarter earlier.

## Product Example

A team planning a requested feature debates a large research programme. The decision
turns on one uncertainty: is this a workflow many customers repeat, or one large
customer's unusual process? Usage data plus eight interviews resolve it in a fortnight.
The remaining research questions are interesting, but none of them could change whether
to build.

## Questions

- What decision is this information for?
- What result would make me choose differently, and how likely is it?
- What is the cost of waiting for the answer, not just of obtaining it?
- Would even a perfect answer be worth the delay?

## Common Misuse

The idea is abused to dismiss evidence that is inconvenient: "it wouldn't change
anything" is easy to claim when the conclusion is already chosen. It also understates
information whose value is protective — safety checks, legal review and the risk of a
rare catastrophic outcome are often low in probability and high in consequence, and a
quick calculation will undervalue them. And it assumes you know which decisions lie
ahead; some knowledge is valuable because it improves choices you cannot yet see.
