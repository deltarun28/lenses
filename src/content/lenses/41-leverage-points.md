## Principle

Where you intervene in a system matters more than how hard you push. A small change to a
rule, an information flow or a goal can outlast a large addition of resources.

## Explanation

When an outcome keeps recurring, more effort usually treats the symptom. Overtime clears
a backlog that refills; a campaign lifts a number that drifts back. The recurrence is
the clue: something in the structure — who decides, what is rewarded, who sees which
information, and when — keeps regenerating the result.

The systems scientist Donella Meadows ranked places to intervene, from the weakest
(adjusting quantities, such as budgets and headcount) through stronger ones (the speed
of feedback, who has access to information, the rules) to the strongest (the system's
goal, and the assumptions it is built on). Her ranking is an argument from experience,
not a measured result, but its central observation holds up well in practice: the
parameters people argue about most are usually the ones that change least.

Leverage points differ from the binding constraint in Theory of Constraints. The
constraint tells you where output is limited. A leverage point tells you which change
would alter how the whole system behaves — and it is frequently not where the problem is
visible. A useful test is the one Meadows implied: if you could not add time, money or
people, what would you change?

## Military Example

A maintenance unit repeatedly misses vehicle availability targets. Overtime improves a
week or two, then the backlog returns. The investigation finds that crews can submit
incomplete fault reports, so technicians spend the first part of every job rediscovering
basic information. Requiring a short, standard fault description at intake changes the
handoff and removes rework before it enters the queue. No extra technicians were needed;
the information flow was the lever.

## Leadership Example

A service team keeps escalating minor requests because nobody is authorised to decline
them. Hiring another coordinator would only process more low-value work faster. The
leader instead defines a value threshold and gives frontline staff a clear way to
decline below it. Demand changes at the entry point, and the queue becomes manageable
without extra headcount.

## Product Example

Support tickets rise after every release, and the team proposes another support rota.
Tracing the flow shows that release notes reach customer-facing staff only after
deployment, so they learn of changes from customers. Publishing a short change brief
before release gives support the missing information, and the post-release spike largely
disappears.

## Questions

- What outcome does this system keep producing, despite effort to change it?
- Which rule, incentive, information flow or goal most strongly shapes that outcome?
- If I could not add time, money or people, what would I change?
- What intervention would hold without someone continually enforcing it?

## Common Misuse

Leverage-point thinking can become an excuse to skip the practical fix in favour of a
grand structural one. A small visible repair may still be urgent while the structural
work proceeds. The most powerful levers are also the most dangerous: changing a goal or
an incentive alters behaviour in ways nobody fully predicts, and people reliably push
high-leverage points in the wrong direction. Make structural changes small enough to
observe, and keep the causal claim modest until the evidence arrives.
