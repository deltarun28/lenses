## Principle

When people need to coordinate without being able to agree in advance, they converge on
whatever option stands out as the obvious one.

## Explanation

The economist Thomas Schelling asked people where they would go to meet a stranger in
New York if they had no way to communicate. A large share chose Grand Central Station at
noon. Nothing made it the best place; it was simply the answer each person expected the
others to choose. Schelling called such answers focal points, and they are now often
named after him.

Focal points matter wherever coordination is needed and communication is costly, slow or
impossible: in a crisis, across organisational boundaries, between people who will never
meet. The choice does not need to be optimal. It needs to be salient — distinctive,
simple, and recognised by everyone involved.

This gives a practical design principle. You can create focal points deliberately: a
fixed time, a single named place for a kind of information, a round number, a default
that everyone knows applies unless said otherwise. A well-chosen default saves a
negotiation every time it is used.

In negotiation, focal points also anchor outcomes. Round numbers, splitting the
difference, and precedents shape where agreements land, partly because they are easy to
justify to others.

## Military Example

Before an operation, patrols are briefed on a rally point, a time window and a fallback
if the window is missed. When radios fail and teams are separated, each team moves to
the rally point because it knows the others will. The plan works because the defaults
are simple, rehearsed and shared, not because separated teams can negotiate a new
meeting place.

## Leadership Example

A distributed organisation struggles to find out who owns what. Rather than a better
communication campaign, it creates one convention: every team publishes a single page at
a predictable address listing what it owns and how to reach it. Within months people
stop asking around, because everyone knows where to look and expects others to look
there too.

## Product Example

A product scatters billing controls across several settings screens. Customers and
support staff waste time looking in different places. The team moves everything into one
clearly named section and links to it from wherever people previously looked. The
section becomes the obvious place, and billing-related support requests fall.

## Questions

- What would everyone involved expect everyone else to choose?
- Where do we renegotiate the same thing repeatedly that a default could settle?
- Is the focal point we rely on recognised by every group, or only ours?
- Is the convention we have still the right one, or only the familiar one?

## Common Misuse

A focal point is not proof that an option is fair or good. Conventions often reflect who
had the power to set them, and their obviousness makes them hard to question. Defaults
that are obvious to one group can be invisible to another, especially across cultures or
new members. And a focal point can become a trap when conditions change and everyone
still converges on the old answer. Review important conventions when the people or the
situation change.
