## Principle

Use rules where cases are frequent and alike, spend judgement where they are rare and
varied, and make sure judgement is still being developed for the cases that need it.

## Explanation

Every organisation decides, usually without noticing, which decisions are made by rule
and which by judgement. Legal scholars studying "rules versus standards" frame this as a
trade-off in costs. A rule ("the speed limit is 50") is expensive to design and cheap to
apply. A standard ("drive at a reasonable speed") is cheap to write and expensive to
apply, because someone has to exercise judgement every time. Rules win for frequent,
similar cases; judgement wins for rare and varied ones.

Judgement has costs that are easy to underestimate. It needs scarce, experienced people.
It takes time. And it is inconsistent: Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein
describe "noise audits" in which professionals assessing identical cases, such as
insurance underwriters pricing the same risk, disagreed far more than their managers
expected. In many prediction tasks, simple statistical rules match or beat expert
judgement, a finding that goes back to the psychologist Paul Meehl in the 1950s and has
held up in later reviews.

But rules fail at the edges: in cases the rule-writer did not foresee, and where
applying the rule literally defeats its purpose. There, judgement cannot be replaced,
and it is only available if it has been developed. Judgement is built by experience with
feedback, and much of that experience has traditionally come from handling routine
cases. If rules and tools take over all the routine work, the path by which people
acquire judgement can disappear, just as the remaining cases become harder.

So the design question has two parts. Where does variation between cases matter enough
to justify judgement's cost? And where will tomorrow's judgement come from?

## Military Example

Rules of engagement and standard procedures govern well-understood situations, so that
soldiers do not have to improvise under fire. Ambiguous situations — a vehicle
approaching a checkpoint that may or may not be hostile — cannot be fully covered by
rules and depend on trained judgement guided by intent. Training time is therefore
concentrated on ambiguous scenarios, because that is where judgement has to be built.

## Leadership Example

An insurer has senior underwriters review every application. Most are routine, and a
scoring rule handles them more consistently. The underwriters' time moves to unusual
risks. Junior underwriters handle a share of those unusual cases under supervision, so
that the firm is still producing senior underwriters in ten years' time.

## Product Example

A marketplace screens listings for fraud. Clear-cut cases are handled automatically by
rules. Ambiguous cases go to human reviewers. The reviewers also examine a random sample
of the automated decisions, which catches cases where the rules have drifted out of date
and keeps the reviewers in contact with the full range of cases.

## Questions

- Which decisions here are frequent and alike, and which are rare and varied?
- Where are we paying for judgement on cases a rule would handle as well?
- Where is a rule being applied to cases it was never designed for?
- Where will the experienced judgement we need in ten years come from?

## Common Misuse

The argument for rules — cheaper, more consistent, measurably better at many predictions
— can be used to remove discretion everywhere, leaving nobody able to handle the cases
the rules miss. The opposite error is claiming "this needs judgement" to resist evidence
that a simple rule does better. Rules have hidden costs of their own, including rigidity
and gaming. The balance shifts as conditions change, so the split between rules and
judgement needs revisiting rather than settling once.
