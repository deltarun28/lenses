## Principle

When a measure becomes the target, it stops being a good measure.

## Explanation

Metrics exist to describe reality, not replace it. Once people are rewarded for
improving a metric, they naturally optimise the metric — even if the underlying
objective suffers.

Goodhart's Law explains why organisations often hit KPIs while missing their real
goals.

## Military Example

During training, a unit is evaluated on how quickly it can complete a mission. Crews
begin cutting corners on camouflage and reconnaissance to reduce completion times.
Training scores improve, but battlefield effectiveness would likely decline because the
metric no longer reflects combat readiness.

## Leadership Example

A manager is measured on reducing employee turnover. Rather than improving leadership,
they avoid addressing poor performance to keep resignation numbers low. Retention
improves, but overall team effectiveness declines.

## Product Example

A software company optimises for daily active users. Product teams add notifications,
pop-ups, and frictionless re-engagement. Usage increases, but customer satisfaction
falls because the experience becomes intrusive.

## Questions

- What is the metric trying to represent?
- How could someone improve this number without improving the underlying outcome?
- What second metric would reveal whether we are fooling ourselves?

## Common Misuse

Some people conclude that metrics are inherently bad. They are not. Metrics are
essential — but they should guide judgment, not replace it. Strong organisations pair
quantitative measures with qualitative observation.
