## Principle

Ask of any action both what it achieves and what it communicates, because the two can
come apart.

## Explanation

When qualities cannot be observed directly — competence, commitment, honesty, quality —
people infer them from actions. The economist Michael Spence showed in the 1970s why
some signals work: a signal is credible when it is cheaper for someone who has the
quality to send than for someone who does not. A long warranty is credible because a
maker of unreliable products cannot afford to honour it. A claim that costs nothing to
make tells you almost nothing.

Signalling is not a flaw. Much useful coordination depends on it: a qualification, a
public commitment or a costly investment lets others rely on you without checking
everything themselves. The problems begin in two places.

The first is when the signal becomes the goal. A team that is judged by how busy,
thorough or committed it appears will produce appearances: long hours seen, documents
written, meetings attended. The substance the signal was meant to indicate may grow, or
quietly decline.

The second is when observers stop testing whether the signal is still costly. A
credential that once required real skill can become obtainable without it; a public
commitment that once carried a price can become routine. The signal persists after its
connection to the quality has gone.

## Leadership Example

A leader publishes a written record explaining each significant decision, including the
dissenting views. It does practical work: it clarifies ownership and gives future
readers the reasoning. It also signals that disagreement can be recorded without
penalty. That signal stays credible only while the records occasionally show the leader
persuaded to change course. If no record ever does, readers learn that the openness is
decoration.

## Product Example

A software company offers a thirty-day free trial with one-click cancellation. The trial
gives customers real information, and it signals confidence: a company with a weak
product would lose more from easy cancellation than a company with a strong one. A
competitor copies the trial but hides cancellation behind a phone call. It keeps the
appearance and discards both the substance and the credibility.

## Questions

- What does this action actually achieve, setting aside what it shows?
- What does it communicate, and to whom?
- Would someone without the quality find this signal expensive to fake?
- Are we being rewarded for the signal while the substance declines?

## Common Misuse

Explaining all behaviour as signalling is shallow and corrosive; most people who work
hard are working, not performing. The lens can also be turned against useful visible
practices — a thorough review or a careful document is not worthless because others can
see it. And costly signals can still deceive: expensive offices have been the backdrop
to many frauds. Use the distinction to test whether substance is still behind a signal,
not to assume it never is.
