## Principle

Hand on the reason for a rule along with the rule, so that those who inherit it can tell
when it still applies.

## Explanation

Institutions accumulate rules — procedures, standards, policies, doctrine — and each one
usually encodes a lesson someone learned, often at a cost. Over time, the people who
learned the lesson leave and the rule remains. Successors who know only the rule have
two bad options: follow it rigidly where it no longer fits, or discard it as arbitrary.
Chesterton's Fence warns against the second. This lens is about preventing both, from
the side of the people who make and pass on the rules.

A rule passed on with its reason allows intelligent adaptation. The reader can see when
circumstances differ from those the rule was written for, and can serve the purpose
rather than the wording. Commander's Intent applies this to orders. The common-law
tradition applies it to precedent: later courts are bound by the reasoning that decided
a case, not merely its outcome, which allows them to distinguish cases that differ in
ways that matter. Science applies it to knowledge: it keeps its methods of inquiry while
revising its conclusions, and so it can change its mind without losing its way of
deciding.

This suggests what institutional continuity really requires. An organisation that keeps
its rules but forgets their reasons becomes rigid, and then, when the rigidity becomes
intolerable, discards the rules wholesale and relearns the old lessons. An organisation
that keeps its reasoning can change its rules safely, one at a time.

The practical habits are modest: record the reason, the evidence and the conditions when
an important rule is made; review rules when those conditions change; and teach
newcomers the reasons for the rules that matter most, not just the rules.

## Military Example

Range standing orders forbid firing from one position. Newcomers think the restriction
excessive. The orders record why: years earlier, rounds ricocheted from a band of rock
behind the targets. When the targets are later moved, the range staff revise the
restriction correctly, because they know what it was protecting against, rather than
either keeping it out of habit or dropping it as a relic.

## Leadership Example

A company requires two signatures on payments above a threshold. The rule was introduced
after a fraud involving one type of payment, but over the years it has been applied to
everything and the threshold never adjusted. Because the original reason had been
recorded, the company can tighten the control on the risky payment type and relax it
elsewhere, instead of choosing between keeping it all and scrapping it.

## Product Example

An engineering team keeps short decision records for its architectural rules, such as
"never call the billing service synchronously", noting the context and the expected
consequences. Five years later, with new infrastructure, a team reading the record can
see that the original constraint — the billing service's slow responses — no longer
holds, and revises the rule with confidence.

## Questions

- Why does this rule exist, and is the reason written down anywhere?
- Would that reason still apply in today's conditions?
- Are we teaching newcomers the rules, or how to reason about them?
- Which of our conclusions should be open to revision, and which methods must we keep?

## Common Misuse

Not every rule needs a recorded justification; documenting everything becomes overhead
nobody reads. Some rules must be followed without debate in the moment, especially in
emergencies and safety-critical work: knowing the reason is for reviewing the rule, not
for improvising exceptions under pressure. Reasons can also be invented after the fact
to defend a rule someone wants to keep. Record genuine reasons for the rules that
matter, and review them deliberately.
