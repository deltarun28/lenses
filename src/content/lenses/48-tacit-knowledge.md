## Principle

People know more than they can tell, and what cannot be told cannot be fully passed on
in writing.

## Explanation

The philosopher Michael Polanyi put it as "we can know more than we can tell". You
recognise a face without being able to list its features. An experienced engineer hears
that a bearing is failing; a negotiator senses the moment a discussion has turned. A
large share of expertise is like this: built through practice and feedback, used without
conscious reasoning, and very hard to put into words.

There is good evidence for its practical importance. The sociologist Harry Collins
studied laboratories trying to build a new type of laser in the 1970s. Groups working
only from published papers failed; the groups that succeeded had spent time with people
who had already built one. In Ikujiro Nonaka and Hirotaka Takeuchi's well-known account
of the development of a bread-making machine at Matsushita, a member of the development
team trained under a hotel's head baker to learn a kneading technique no recipe
described, then worked with engineers to reproduce it in the machine.

The consequences for organisations are concrete. A procedure written by an expert is not
the expert's expertise; it is the part they could articulate. Tacit knowledge leaves
when people leave, fades when practice stops, and is easily destroyed by
reorganisations, outsourcing and early-retirement schemes, because nobody knew it was
there until it was gone. Some of it can be made explicit with effort, through worked
examples, checklists and recorded reasoning. The rest moves only through overlap:
shadowing, apprenticeship and working side by side.

## Military Example

An experienced vehicle commander can read ground: from the colour of the soil and the
vegetation, they know which tracks will hold after rain and which will bog a vehicle
down. The manual describes soil types. Junior commanders paired with them for a season
of exercises learn to see what they see; a lecture on soil types does not produce the
same skill.

## Leadership Example

A long-serving operations manager retires, and their procedures are carefully
documented. Within months, supplier problems escalate that the manager would have
defused, because they knew which suppliers needed an early call and which contacts
actually acted. A planned overlap of several months, in which a successor shadowed the
manager through real problems, would have transferred far more than the manual.

## Product Example

A team rewrites an old system from its documentation. Dozens of edge cases that the old
code handled implicitly, and that one support engineer knew about from experience, are
lost. For the next migration, the team involves that engineer from the start and runs
the old and new systems in parallel, comparing their outputs to expose behaviour nobody
had written down.

## Questions

- What does our best person know that is not written down anywhere?
- If they left tomorrow, what would we discover we had lost?
- Where are we relying on a document to transfer something that needs practice?
- How do newcomers actually learn this, and do they have the time and access to?

## Common Misuse

"Tacit knowledge" can be used to protect a position from scrutiny — "you wouldn't
understand" — and much of what is claimed to be inexpressible can be written down with
effort, which makes it possible to examine, teach and improve. Tacit knowledge can also
be wrong: expert intuitions are reliable only where they were formed with accurate
feedback. Write down what can be written down, and keep apprenticeship for what
genuinely cannot be.
