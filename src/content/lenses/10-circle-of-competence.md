## Principle

What matters is not how much you know, but whether you know where your knowledge stops.

## Explanation

Everyone has a domain where their intuition is trained and reliable, built from
repeated exposure with real feedback. Inside it, judgement is fast and usually correct.
Outside it, the same confidence persists while the accuracy does not — and it feels
identical from the inside.

The boundary is the valuable part. A narrow circle with a clear edge outperforms a wide
one with a vague edge, because the second produces confident decisions in exactly the
places where you have no basis for confidence.

The edge is found through feedback, not introspection. Where have your predictions been
tested and proven right? Where have you never actually found out? Deference outside the
circle is not modesty; it is accuracy about the source of your judgement.

## Military Example

An officer with extensive experience in one theatre and terrain type arrives in a
different one. The tactical fundamentals transfer. Judgements about tempo, local
relationships and what a particular pattern of activity signifies do not — those were
built on a specific population and a specific adversary. The competent response is to
weight local advice heavily for a period, and to notice which of one's instincts are
being confirmed and which are not.

## Leadership Example

An executive promoted from engineering keeps making the final call on technical
architecture. The knowledge is real but four years stale, and the team has better
current information. The valuable contribution — deciding what the organisation should
optimise for, and who should own the decision — sits inside the circle. The
architectural call sits outside it, and taking it removes both accuracy and ownership.

## Product Example

A founder with strong instincts in one market enters an adjacent one and applies the
same assumptions about buying behaviour and sales cycle. The product intuition that was
right twenty times in a row is now untested. The signal is direct: the usual heuristics
stop predicting outcomes, and the correct response is to run cheap tests rather than to
trust a track record earned elsewhere.

## Questions

- Where has my judgement been tested against reality, and where has it not?
- Am I reasoning from experience here, or from confidence built somewhere else?
- Who has genuine competence in this area, and am I actually weighting their view?
- Has anything changed since my experience was earned?

## Common Misuse

The concept is used to avoid learning: declaring something outside the circle is an
easy way to never expand it, and circles do grow with deliberate work. It also gets
used to dismiss outsiders, whose lack of embedded assumptions is sometimes exactly what
a problem needs. And it is a claim about judgement, not authority — being inside your
circle does not settle a question where others are also inside theirs.
