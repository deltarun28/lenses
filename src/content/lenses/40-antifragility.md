## Principle

Distinguish what breaks under stress, what merely survives it, and what actually gets
better for it.

## Explanation

Three distinct responses are usually collapsed into one. The fragile is harmed by
volatility. The robust is unaffected. The antifragile improves — muscle under load,
immune systems under exposure, a business that gains share in a downturn because it
holds cash.

The distinction matters because the design choices differ. Fragility is removed by
adding margin. Antifragility requires a structure where small stresses produce
information or advantage, and where the downside is capped so that no single event ends
the exercise.

That capped downside is the whole mechanism. Frequent small failures that teach
something, with a floor under how bad any one can be, produce improvement. The same
exposure without the floor is just risk.

## Military Example

A force that trains under realistic stress — degraded communications, failed equipment,
unexpected orders — treats each exercise failure as information and improves from it.
The exercise is designed with a floor: a failed serial costs a rerun, not a loss. A force
that only trains under favourable conditions looks better on every measurement and
discovers its fragility once, at the worst possible time.

## Leadership Example

An organisation that surfaces small problems continually and acts on them gets stronger
with each one. An organisation that suppresses them accumulates undetected fragility and
appears stable right up until something arrives too large to suppress. Both look calm
from outside; only one is improving, and the difference is visible only in how they
handle the first genuine shock.

## Product Example

A team practising frequent small deploys with fast rollback experiences failures weekly,
each cheap and each improving the system. A team deploying quarterly experiences failure
rarely and catastrophically, with no practised recovery. The first team has more
incidents and less risk — the frequency is what builds the capability.

## Questions

- Does volatility here damage us, leave us unaffected, or teach us something?
- Is the downside of any single failure genuinely capped?
- Are we experiencing small stresses regularly, or avoiding them until one is large?
- Does our stability come from strength or from suppression?

## Common Misuse

The term gets applied to anything that merely survives adversity, which is robustness,
and the difference is the whole idea. It also romanticises stress: most stress is simply
damaging, and people, teams and systems are broken by it far more often than improved.
The conditions are specific — small, frequent, recoverable, with feedback — and absent
any of those, "what doesn't kill you" is just harm.
