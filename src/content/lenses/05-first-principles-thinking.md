## Principle

Separate what is physically or logically necessary from what is merely customary, and
rebuild from the necessary parts.

## Explanation

Most reasoning is by analogy: this resembles something we have seen, so we do roughly
what was done then. Analogy is fast and usually adequate, which is why it dominates. It
also carries forward every constraint that applied to the original situation, including
the ones that no longer exist.

First principles reasoning is the deliberate, expensive alternative. Take the problem
apart until you reach statements that must be true — physical limits, contractual
obligations, arithmetic — and treat everything else as a choice someone made. Then ask
whether you would make the same choice today.

It is slow, so it is worth reserving for decisions that are expensive, repeated, or
locked in for years. Applied to everything, it wastes the accumulated judgement that
convention represents.

## Military Example

A resupply cycle runs on a seven-day rhythm because that is how the previous operation
was run. Broken down, the actual constraints are vehicle range, threat on the route,
and consumption rate for two commodities. Those constraints imply a different pattern
for each commodity — frequent light lifts for one, infrequent heavy lifts for the other.
The seven-day cycle was never a requirement; it was a habit that outlived the situation
that produced it.

## Leadership Example

An organisation runs a nine-month performance cycle because that is standard practice.
Reduced to necessities, the requirements are: people need to know where they stand,
pay decisions need a defensible basis, and poor performance needs a documented path.
None of these require an annual form. Two require regular conversations, and one
requires a written record — a design that looks nothing like the process being copied.

## Product Example

A team assumes a mobile app is needed because competitors have one. The underlying
requirements are: access on a phone, availability without a stable connection, and a
home-screen icon. In this case a well-built progressive web app satisfies all three at
a fraction of the cost, with no app store review cycle. The native app was an analogy,
not a requirement.

## Questions

- Which parts of this are physical or contractual constraints, and which are conventions?
- If this did not already exist, how would we build it today?
- Who decided the thing I am treating as fixed, and what did they know?
- Is this decision expensive or durable enough to justify reasoning from scratch?

## Common Misuse

The characteristic failure is arrogance: treating hard-won convention as arbitrary and
rediscovering, at cost, why the old approach existed. This is where Chesterton's Fence
belongs alongside it — understand the reason for the constraint before removing it. The
second failure is scope. Applied to every decision, first principles reasoning is
paralysing and produces worse outcomes than competent copying. Use it where the stakes
justify the cost.
