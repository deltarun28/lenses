## Principle

Improvement often comes more reliably from taking something away than from adding
something new.

## Explanation

When something is not working, the instinctive response is to add: another meeting, an
approval step, a tool, a metric, a feature. Additions are visible and feel like action.
Removal feels like loss, or like admitting the original was wrong.

This bias is measurable. In a series of experiments published in _Nature_ in 2021,
Gabrielle Adams and colleagues gave people problems — improving a structure, an essay,
an itinerary — that could be solved by adding or by removing elements. People
overwhelmingly reached for additions and frequently missed better subtractive solutions,
especially when they were busy or not prompted to consider removal. The fix was simple:
asking explicitly what could be taken away.

The term comes from theology, where it meant describing God by what God is not; the
writer Nassim Taleb popularised it as a principle for decisions. Its practical logic is
that every element carries ongoing costs: maintenance, coordination, attention, and new
ways to fail. Removing an element removes those costs as well as the element. And
knowledge of what harms is often more reliable than knowledge of what helps — it is
easier to be confident that a step causes errors than that a new step will prevent them.

## Military Example

A headquarters has accumulated four daily briefings. Staff spend hours preparing
overlapping slides, and the commander's decisions still wait on the same two facts. The
chief of staff removes three briefings and keeps one short update built around the
decisions due that day. Staff time returns to planning, and situational awareness does
not fall.

## Leadership Example

A team responds to quality problems by proposing another approval stage. Examining the
process shows that one existing handoff, where work is re-keyed between systems, causes
most of the errors. Removing the re-keying improves quality more than an extra layer of
review would have, and adds no delay.

## Product Example

An onboarding flow has grown a step with each release. Analysis shows that one
permission request, added for a feature few people use, causes most abandonment.
Removing it, and asking for the permission only when that feature is first used,
improves completion more than any of the proposed new tutorials.

## Questions

- What could we remove instead of adding something?
- What exists here only because nobody has ever taken it away?
- Which element costs more in maintenance and failure than it delivers?
- If we were starting from scratch, would we add this?

## Common Misuse

Subtraction removes safeguards as easily as waste, and a control built for a rare but
serious failure looks useless in every ordinary week. That is Chesterton's Fence: find
out why something exists before taking it down. Removal can also become an aesthetic —
minimalism or cost-cutting pursued for its own sake, with the outcome forgotten. And
sometimes the honest answer is that something is missing. Ask the subtractive question
first, not instead of every other question.
