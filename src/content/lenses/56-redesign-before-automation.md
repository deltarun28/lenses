## Principle

Before automating a process, ask how the work would be designed if the new capability
had existed from the start.

## Explanation

The economic historian Paul David observed that electric motors spread through American
factories from the 1890s, but the large productivity gains did not appear until the
1920s. Early electrified factories replaced their central steam engine with one large
electric motor and kept everything else: the multi-storey building, the overhead shafts
and belts, the layout dictated by mechanical power transmission. The gains came when
factories were rebuilt around small motors on each machine, on a single floor, arranged
by the flow of work. That took decades and, often, new factories built by new people.

Economists have found similar delays with computers, and describe a "productivity
J-curve": the benefits of a general-purpose technology arrive only after complementary
investments in processes, skills and organisation, which at first look like costs.
Michael Hammer made the managerial version of the argument in 1990, in an essay titled
"Don't Automate, Obliterate": automating an existing process mostly makes unnecessary
steps faster.

There are three reasons inserted technology underdelivers. Existing processes are shaped
by old constraints — paper moving between desks, approvals that existed because
information travelled slowly — and automating them preserves the constraint's logic
after the constraint itself has gone. Speeding up one step moves the bottleneck
elsewhere, so overall output barely changes. And existing processes depend on informal
workarounds that nobody documented, which the new system quietly breaks.

The same pattern has recurred with each wave of technology, and is recurring now with
artificial intelligence. It is a property of organisations, not of any particular tool.

## Military Example

A headquarters introduces digital messaging but keeps its paper procedures: messages are
printed, signed by three officers, scanned and sent. Transmission is instant; approval
still takes hours. The real gain comes when the headquarters redesigns approval levels,
delegates release authority for routine traffic, and shares information directly with
the people who need it.

## Leadership Example

An organisation moves expense claims from paper to an online system with the same five
approval steps. Processing time barely changes. Redesign follows: claims under a
threshold that comply with policy are approved automatically and audited at random
afterwards, and approvers see only the exceptions. Reimbursement time falls from weeks
to days.

## Product Example

A support team adds a tool that drafts replies inside its existing ticket queue. Agents
spend much of the saved time correcting drafts, and the gain is small. The team then
looks at why customers write in at all, fixes the three most common causes in the
product, and reorganises the queue so experienced agents handle the complex cases. The
volume of tickets falls, which no drafting tool could have achieved.

## Questions

- Which steps exist only because of a limitation the new tool removes?
- If we designed this work from scratch with the new capability, what would it look like?
- When this step gets faster, where will the bottleneck move?
- What undocumented workarounds does the current process depend on?

## Common Misuse

Redesign is expensive, disruptive and risky, and many large reengineering programmes of
the 1990s failed or became cover for cutting jobs. Inserting a tool into the existing
process can be a sensible first step to learn what it can do before redesigning around
it. Existing processes also encode lessons that are invisible until removed;
Chesterton's Fence applies. Redesign in stages, with the people who do the work, and
keep what turns out to be load-bearing.
