## Principle

What you expect of someone changes how you treat them, and that treatment can produce
the performance you expected.

## Explanation

In 1968 Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson reported that pupils whom teachers had been
told (falsely, at random) were about to "bloom" made larger gains than their classmates.
The study became famous and was heavily criticised for its methods. Decades of later
research suggest the effect is real but usually modest, often fades, and is strongest
when the person forming the expectation knows little else about the individual. Studies
by Dov Eden in military training found similar effects for trainees whose instructors
expected more of them.

The mechanism is not mysterious, and it is the useful part. Expectations change
behaviour in observable ways. Someone thought promising gets harder assignments, more
detailed feedback, more patience after mistakes and more introductions. Someone thought
weak gets routine work, less attention and a shorter leash. After a year, the difference
in performance is partly a difference in what each was given — and it looks like
confirmation of the original judgement.

So the practical question is not whether to have high expectations in the abstract. It
is whether your early judgements are shaping access to the things that build capability:
stretching work, coaching, visibility and second chances.

## Military Example

An instructor forms an early view of which recruits are natural leaders and gives them
the section-commander roles in exercises. Those recruits get practice leading; the
others get practice following. At the end of the course, the leadership assessments
confirm the instructor's early view. A rotation that gives every recruit a turn in
command produces evidence about leadership rather than about the instructor's first
impressions.

## Leadership Example

A manager has concluded that one team member has limited potential and gives them
routine tasks. Reviewing a year's assignments, the manager notices that this person has
had no work that could have shown otherwise. The manager assigns a bounded, visible
project with coaching and clear success criteria. The result is real evidence, in either
direction, in place of a label.

## Product Example

A product team assumes less technical customers will not use an advanced feature, so it
hides it and writes no guidance. Low usage among those customers seems to confirm the
assumption. A small trial with clear examples shows many of them use it readily. The
original expectation had produced the outcome it was supposed to predict.

## Questions

- What do I expect of this person, and when did I form that view?
- How has that expectation shaped the work and feedback they get?
- Have they had a real opportunity to prove the expectation wrong?
- What evidence would change my mind, and am I collecting it?

## Common Misuse

The popular version overstates the evidence and promises that belief alone transforms
performance; it does not, and expecting more without providing support simply sets
people up to fail. It can also be turned into blame — if expectations shape outcomes,
the person who did not improve must not have been believed in enough. Use the effect to
audit how opportunities are distributed, while keeping standards, honest feedback and
skill-building intact.
