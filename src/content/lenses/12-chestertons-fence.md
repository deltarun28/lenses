## Principle

Before removing a constraint that looks pointless, find out what it was protecting
against.

## Explanation

New arrivals see waste that insiders have stopped noticing, and that fresh perspective
is genuinely valuable. It also carries a specific failure: rules that look arbitrary
often encode an expensive lesson nobody wrote down.

The discipline is a single step, not a defence of the status quo. Find out why it
exists. If the reason has gone, remove it with more confidence than before. If the
reason is still live, you have avoided rediscovering it the hard way. If nobody can
explain it — which is common — that is information too, and the honest response is to
remove it deliberately while watching for what breaks.

The cost of the check is usually one conversation with someone who has been there long
enough to remember.

## Military Example

A standing order requires vehicles to halt short of a particular junction before
crossing. It costs time on every move and appears to serve no purpose. The reason is
that a previous convoy was engaged there, and the halt exists to allow observation of
ground that cannot be seen on approach. The threat may well have gone — but that is a
question to answer, not to assume, and the answer is held by people still present.

## Leadership Example

A new manager removes a mandatory sign-off that adds three days to every hire. Hiring
speeds up. Six months later a series of appointments are made outside the approved pay
bands, because the sign-off was the only control on that, and it was invisible in the
process description. The control was badly designed and worth replacing. Removing it
without knowing what it did was the error.

## Product Example

An engineer deletes a retry loop that looks redundant, and throughput improves. Two
weeks later, an intermittent upstream failure that the loop had been silently absorbing
for years begins surfacing as customer-visible errors. Nothing in the code explained
the loop, which is a real deficiency — but the check was one query to whoever wrote it.

## Questions

- Why does this exist, and who would know?
- What would happen the first time the original condition recurs?
- Is the original reason still true, or has the environment changed?
- If nobody can explain it, can I remove it in a way that reveals what breaks?

## Common Misuse

The fence becomes a veto. Any change can be delayed indefinitely by demanding a full
account of a rule nobody remembers writing, and organisations use it exactly that way.
The principle requires an _inquiry_, not permanent deference — and the inquiry is
bounded. If a reasonable effort produces no explanation, remove it and observe. Standing
processes should have to justify themselves too.
