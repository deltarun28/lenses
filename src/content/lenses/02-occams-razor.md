## Principle

Among competing explanations, start with the one that assumes the least.

## Explanation

Occam's Razor is a tiebreaker for diagnosis, not a claim that reality is simple. Each
assumption in an explanation is a place it can be wrong, so an explanation resting on
five uncertain conditions is far less likely to be correct than one resting on one —
even when the complicated story is more interesting.

Its real utility is ordering your investigation. The cheap, boring hypothesis gets
tested first. If it survives, you have your answer at low cost. If it fails, you have
eliminated it and earned the right to consider something more elaborate.

The razor is about priority, not truth. Complicated explanations are sometimes correct.
They just have to earn their place.

## Military Example

A patrol reports intermittent radio contact in a valley. The elaborate reading is
electronic interference from an adversary. The simple readings are terrain masking, a
low battery, or an antenna seated badly. Signals staff check the equipment and the
ground before escalating. Treating the sophisticated explanation as the default would
have committed analysts and time to a threat picture built on one unverified symptom.

## Leadership Example

A manager notices a normally reliable team member missing deadlines and going quiet in
meetings. The complex story involves disengagement, a competing job offer, and a
grievance about the last promotion round. The simple story is that their workload
changed two months ago and nobody adjusted their commitments. A single conversation
distinguishes the two, and it should happen before any theory of motive is acted on.

## Product Example

Sign-ups drop sharply overnight. The team starts theorising about a competitor launch
and a shift in the market. The simpler candidates — a broken deployment, an expired
certificate, an analytics tag removed in the last release, a pricing page returning an
error on one browser — are all checkable within an hour. One of them is usually the
answer, and the market theory would have cost a week.

## Questions

- What is the most boring explanation that fits every fact I have?
- How many separate things must be true for my current theory to hold?
- Which candidate explanation is cheapest to rule out?
- Am I preferring the complicated story because it is more flattering or more interesting?

## Common Misuse

The razor is regularly used to shut down analysis rather than order it: "simplest is
best" becomes a way to dismiss evidence that does not fit. It also gets confused with
"most familiar". An explanation you already believe is not simpler — it is just closer
to hand. And in genuinely complex systems, the simplest explanation is often wrong;
several causes interact. Use it to sequence your checks, not to decide when to stop
looking.
