## Principle

Agreement among people is strong evidence only when their judgements were formed
independently.

## Explanation

Groups can be more accurate than their individual members, under one condition. In 1907
Francis Galton analysed about eight hundred guesses of an ox's weight at a country fair;
the median guess was within about one per cent of the true weight, closer than most
individual guesses. The Marquis de Condorcet had shown in the eighteenth century that a
majority vote of independent voters, each more likely right than wrong, is more reliable
than any one of them. Both results depend on independence. Errors cancel out only when
they point in different directions.

When independence fails, so does the benefit. In an experiment by Jan Lorenz and
colleagues, letting people see each other's estimates narrowed the range of answers
without making the group more accurate, while making people more confident. In
organisations, the same shared sources reduce independence constantly: common training,
the same data, the same consultants and models, the same meetings, and the same senior
person speaking first.

This is the cost side of Shared Frameworks. An organisation can become steadily more
efficient at being wrong together. The signs are recognisable: consensus arrives
quickly, everyone cites the same source, nobody can state the strongest case against the
view, and an outside surprise catches everyone in the same way. Some analysts have
argued that widespread reliance on similar risk models added to the scale of the 2008
financial crisis; that is an interpretation rather than a settled finding, but it
illustrates the mechanism.

The remedies preserve independence where errors would be costly: gather estimates before
discussion, seek views from people with different training or information, estimate the
same quantity by different methods, and treat informed dissent as data.

## Military Example

An assessment reports that several intelligence agencies agree a threat is imminent.
Checking the sources shows that all of them depend on the same informant. The agreement
is one source counted several times. Intelligence practice therefore checks whether
sources are independent, and uses deliberate alternative analysis to test the leading
view.

## Leadership Example

Every member of a company's board came from the same industry, and several from the same
consultancy. Their agreement on a market's direction is quick and confident. A newly
appointed director from another sector asks a question nobody had considered. The board
commissions an independent forecast built by a different method before committing.

## Product Example

Three teams produce "independent" demand forecasts for a new product, and they agree
closely. All three were built from the same spreadsheet of market-size assumptions.
Actual demand is less than half the forecast. For the next launch, the forecasts must
use different methods: one bottom-up from customer interviews, one from the size of the
market, and one from the results of past launches.

## Questions

- Are these views independent, or do they share a source?
- What would someone with different training or information conclude?
- Did we collect views before discussing them?
- Is our confidence coming from evidence, or from agreement?

## Common Misuse

Diversity is not automatically accuracy: adding people who are simply uninformed adds
noise, not independent knowledge. Agreement can also be correct; experts often agree
because the evidence is clear. Treating every consensus as suspect makes decisions
impossible and rewards contrarianism for its own sake. The aim is independence where
errors would be costly, not disagreement everywhere.
