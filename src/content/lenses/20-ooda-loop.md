## Principle

Competitive advantage comes from moving through the decision cycle faster and more
accurately than your opponent.

## Explanation

Every actor in a contested situation runs the same loop: observe what is happening,
orient by interpreting it against experience and expectation, decide, and act — which
changes the situation and starts the loop again.

The insight is relative rather than absolute. If your cycle is meaningfully faster than
your opponent's, they are responding to a situation that no longer exists. Their
decisions are correct for the world they observed and wrong for the world they are in,
and the gap compounds.

Orientation is the part that decides the outcome and the part most often skipped. Fast
observation and fast action built on a wrong interpretation produce confident errors at
speed. Speed is only an advantage once orientation is sound.

## Military Example

A smaller force operating with mission-type orders can decide and act at the level where
information arrives, while an opponent routes every decision through a headquarters for
approval. Both may be equally competent. The one whose cycle takes hours rather than
days is repeatedly acting on a current picture against an opponent acting on yesterday's
— which is why decision authority is pushed down rather than centralised.

## Leadership Example

Two organisations face the same regulatory change. One holds a monthly steering meeting
where the decision waits for the next slot; the other has a named decision-maker who can
act within a week. The second is not more intelligent. It completes four cycles of
adjustment while the first completes one, and its fourth decision is informed by three
rounds of real feedback.

## Product Example

A team that ships weekly and instruments what happens learns from four real experiments
a month. A team on a quarterly release makes its bets once and finds out in twelve
weeks. Over a year the difference is not 3 times the output — it is 3 times the number
of corrections, applied to a picture of the market that keeps getting more accurate.

## Questions

- How long is our full cycle from observing something to acting on it?
- Where is the delay — observation, interpretation, approval, or execution?
- Is our orientation actually correct, or are we just moving quickly?
- Who has to approve this, and do they have better information than the person on the ground?

## Common Misuse

The loop is routinely reduced to "be fast", which drops the part that matters. Speed
built on poor orientation just reaches the wrong place sooner, and organisations that
optimise cycle time alone produce a lot of confident churn. It also does not apply
everywhere: irreversible decisions, safety-critical work and anything with a long
feedback delay reward deliberation, not tempo. Cycle fast where mistakes are cheap and
recoverable.
