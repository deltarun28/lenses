## Principle

Before reasoning about the specifics of your case, find out how similar cases have
turned out.

## Explanation

Faced with a forecast, people reason from the inside: the details of this plan, this
team, these circumstances. The inside view feels rigorous because it engages with real
information, and it is reliably optimistic, because the details you can see are the
ones you have already accounted for.

The outside view asks a duller question: what happened to the last hundred things like
this? Projects of this type overrun by this much. Companies at this stage fail at this
rate. It ignores everything that makes your case feel distinctive, and it consistently
forecasts better.

Use both. Start from the base rate, then adjust for genuinely unusual features of your
case — and adjust less than feels right, because everyone believes their case is the
exception.

## Military Example

An operation is planned on the assumption that a river crossing takes a specified
number of hours. The planning figure comes from exercises under favourable conditions.
The relevant base rate is how long crossings of this type have actually taken on
operations, including the ones that went badly. That number is substantially larger,
and building the timeline on the exercise figure means every subsequent phase is
already late.

## Leadership Example

A leader estimates that an integration following an acquisition will take nine months.
The estimate is built from a detailed plan. The outside view — how long comparable
integrations at comparable organisations have taken — says eighteen. The detailed plan
is not evidence against the base rate, because every one of those eighteen-month
integrations also began with a detailed nine-month plan.

## Product Example

A team forecasts that a new product will reach a given adoption level in a year, based
on the strength of the idea and the size of the market. The base rate is what happened
to the last ten products this team, or this company, launched. If the median reached a
tenth of that number, the forecast needs a specific, testable reason why this one
differs — not simply more confidence.

## Questions

- What is the reference class, and what usually happens to members of it?
- What is the base rate before I consider anything about my specific case?
- What concretely makes this case different, and is that difference measurable?
- Whose track record is this estimate built on?

## Common Misuse

Base rates get chosen to suit the argument — a reference class drawn narrowly enough
will support any conclusion, so the class has to be defined before the answer is known.
They also become an excuse for fatalism: genuine changes in conditions do make history
a poor guide, and a base rate drawn from a different technological or regulatory era
can mislead badly. It is the starting point for the estimate, not the end of it.
