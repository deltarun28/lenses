## Principle

Whenever one party acts on another's behalf, their interests differ — and the agent
knows more about what they are doing than the principal does.

## Explanation

Two conditions create the problem: different objectives, and asymmetric information.
Either alone is manageable. Together they mean the principal cannot tell whether a poor
outcome was bad luck or misaligned effort, and the agent knows this.

It is not a claim that people are dishonest. Most agents act reasonably given what they
are measured on and what they personally bear. A recruiter paid per placement, an
adviser paid on transactions, a manager assessed on their own department — each
responds sensibly to their situation, and each produces decisions the principal would
not have chosen.

The remedies are limited and all imperfect: change what the agent is measured on, make
the agent bear some of the outcome, or reduce the information gap. Monitoring alone
usually fails, because the agent controls what gets reported.

## Military Example

A contractor is paid by availability hours for a maintained fleet. Availability is
reported by the contractor and measured against a definition negotiated in advance. The
incentive is to maximise the measured figure — which favours keeping easy platforms
serviceable over restoring the hardest ones, even when those are the ones the operation
depends on. The contract, not the contractor, produced the result.

## Leadership Example

A department head is assessed on their own unit's performance and asked to release a
strong performer to a higher-priority team elsewhere. Doing so is right for the
organisation and costly for them personally, and only they know how essential that
person really is. Appeals to shared purpose will lose to the assessment structure over
time.

## Product Example

An agency is paid a monthly retainer to run acquisition. They report on channels they
control, using attribution they configure. The interest is in demonstrating
attributable volume, not in the customers being profitable eighteen months later. The
principal cannot easily distinguish the two, and by the time the difference shows in
retention, the budget has been spent.

## Questions

- What is this person actually measured on, and how does it differ from what I want?
- Who holds information I cannot verify?
- Does the agent bear any of the downside if this goes badly?
- Would a reasonable person in their position make the same choice?

## Common Misuse

The framing invites suspicion of anyone acting on your behalf, which is expensive: most
delegation works, and treating every agent as a risk destroys the trust that makes
delegation worthwhile in the first place. The other error is believing incentives can be
fully aligned. They cannot — every contract is incomplete, and over-engineering the
measurement usually produces Goodhart's Law instead of alignment.
