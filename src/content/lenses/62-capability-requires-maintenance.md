## Principle

Capabilities decay when they are not exercised, so a capability you have not used
recently is one you are assuming rather than one you have.

## Explanation

Skill decay is well documented. Reviews of the research find substantial loss of trained
skills after periods without use, growing with the length of the gap, and faster for
procedures with many discrete steps than for continuous physical skills. Resuscitation
is a familiar case: studies repeatedly find that people's ability to perform it
correctly declines noticeably within months of training, which is why regular refreshers
are recommended.

Automation creates a particular version of the problem. Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 paper
"Ironies of Automation" pointed out that when a system takes over routine operation, its
operators get less practice, yet they are expected to take over in the rarest and
hardest situations, when the automation cannot cope. Aviation has wrestled with this for
decades; regulators have encouraged airlines to give pilots more opportunity for manual
flying, because skills that automation spares in normal flight are the ones a failure
demands.

Organisational capabilities decay in the same way, and less visibly. A crisis plan that
has never been rehearsed, a supplier relationship that has not been used, a backup never
restored, a skill outsourced years ago: each exists on paper until the day it is needed.
Decay gives no warning, because the only test is use.

So maintenance is a recurring cost, and it looks like waste in every period in which
nothing goes wrong — which is why it is cut. The useful discipline is to decide which
capabilities genuinely need to be kept, at what level, and how you will know they still
work.

## Military Example

A force has not conducted a large-scale river crossing for a decade, but still lists it
among its capabilities. The first exercise in years reveals missing equipment, lapsed
qualifications and nobody who has planned one. Regular exercises, expensive and
unglamorous, are what the capability actually consists of; the entry in the list is only
a claim.

## Leadership Example

A company's disaster recovery plan was written five years ago. When it is finally
tested, half the named contacts have left, two key systems have been replaced, and the
alternative site's access cards have expired. The company assigns an owner to the plan
and schedules a yearly exercise, with findings fixed before the next one.

## Product Example

A service relies on automatic failover between data centres. The manual procedure for
doing it has never been used. When the automation itself fails, nobody remembers the
steps, and the recovery takes hours. The team starts performing a manual failover every
quarter, deliberately, so the procedure and the people stay current.

## Questions

- Which capabilities do we claim that we have not exercised recently?
- How would we know if this skill or plan had decayed?
- What practice did automation or outsourcing remove, and what replaces it?
- Which capabilities are worth their maintenance cost, and which should we retire deliberately?

## Common Misuse

Not every capability deserves to be maintained. Trying to keep everything ready is
expensive, and retiring a capability consciously, with its consequences understood, is a
legitimate decision. Drills can also become rituals that rehearse the easy path and
declare success. And maintaining an obsolete capability is waste: maintenance keeps a
capability current, but it does not tell you whether you still need it.
