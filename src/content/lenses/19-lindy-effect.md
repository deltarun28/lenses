## Principle

Ideas, tools and institutions that have lasted a long time will probably last longer
than recent ones.

## Explanation

People and machines have an expected lifespan that shortens with age. Non-perishable
things — a technology, a format, a body of practice — behave in the opposite way. Each
additional year of survival is evidence of fitness for a range of conditions, including
ones you have not seen.

The reasoning is statistical rather than mystical. Anything that has been in use for
thirty years has survived multiple shifts in fashion, technology and economics.
Something introduced last year has survived nothing, and most of its category will not
be here in five years.

The practical use is asymmetric. It is a strong argument for choosing durable
foundations, and a weak argument about which specific new thing will fail. It also does
not apply to anything with a genuine expiry mechanism.

## Military Example

Doctrine that has survived several generations of technological change — the principles
governing surprise, concentration and simplicity of plan — has been tested against
conditions its authors could not anticipate. Techniques tied to a specific piece of
equipment have not. When the two conflict during planning, the older layer is usually
the safer default, and the burden of proof sits with the newer one.

## Leadership Example

An organisation is choosing between a management practice used continuously for
decades across many industries and one introduced by a consultancy three years ago. The
older practice has survived recessions, cultural shifts and many attempts to replace
it. The new one has survived a favourable market and a good sales pitch. That is not a
reason to reject it, but it does say which one carries the risk.

## Product Example

A team picks the storage layer for a system expected to run for a decade. A relational
database with thirty years of production use, documentation and available expertise is
Lindy. A datastore released eighteen months ago with strong benchmarks is not. The new
one may be better on every technical axis today; the older one is far more likely to
still be maintained, hireable for, and understood in 2036.

## Questions

- How long has this been in continuous use, and through what conditions?
- Is this the kind of thing that ages, or the kind that accumulates evidence by surviving?
- What would I need to believe for the new option to outlast the old one?
- Am I choosing this because it is better, or because it is current?

## Common Misuse

The effect is regularly used to defend whatever exists, which is not what it says —
plenty of long-lived practices are actively harmful and persist for reasons other than
fitness, particularly where they benefit incumbents. It also says nothing about which
option is better now, only about which is likely to persist. And it does not apply to
anything with a real expiry: a technology with a known end-of-support date, or a market
position under active attack, gets no protection from its age.
