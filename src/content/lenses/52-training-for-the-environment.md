## Principle

Practise under the conditions in which the skill will actually be needed, because skill
learned in one setting carries over poorly to another.

## Explanation

Skill is more specific than it feels. Learning research repeatedly finds that
performance depends on how well the conditions of practice match the conditions of use,
a principle psychologists call transfer-appropriate processing. A procedure rehearsed
calmly, with complete information, working equipment and no interruptions, often falls
apart under time pressure, noise, fatigue, ambiguity or missing information, which are
the conditions in which it is usually needed.

A less obvious finding is that the training that looks best can transfer worst. In
experiments on motor skills, practising one task repeatedly produced faster improvement
during training than mixing tasks; but the mixed practice produced better retention and
performance on new variations. The psychologist Robert Bjork calls such conditions
"desirable difficulties": they slow apparent progress and improve real learning.
Training judged by how smoothly it goes is optimising the wrong thing.

The practical step is to list what makes the task hard in reality — stress,
interruptions, degraded tools, incomplete information, unusual cases — and to put those
features into practice, at a level people can learn from. Aviation's line-oriented
flight training does this: whole crews fly realistic scenarios in a simulator, with
failures arriving amid ordinary workload, rather than practising isolated manoeuvres.

This lens and The Economics of Judgement reinforce each other. Judgement grows only
where people meet realistic situations and get feedback on their decisions. Training is
the one place an organisation can provide that environment deliberately, rather than
waiting for real events to provide it at full cost.

## Military Example

A unit practises casualty evacuation in daylight, on flat ground, with every radio
working. In an exercise at night, in rain, with one radio net down, the evacuation takes
three times as long and two steps are missed. The training is redesigned: night serials,
degraded communications and casualties who appear without warning. Times improve
steadily over the season, and the after-action reviews start finding new problems
instead of the same ones.

## Leadership Example

New managers are trained with presentations on policy and process. Their first difficult
conversation with a struggling employee goes badly. The organisation replaces part of
the course with rehearsed conversations with a trained actor, who responds as real
people do, with anger, silence or tears. Managers report their first real conversations
going markedly better.

## Product Example

An engineering team has written procedures for responding to incidents, but has never
used them under pressure. During a real outage at three in the morning, nobody can find
the access credentials the procedure assumes. The team starts running regular exercises
in which a controlled failure is introduced without notice, and fixes what each exercise
exposes.

## Questions

- What makes this task hard in real life, and how much of it is present in training?
- Are we judging training by how smoothly it runs, or by how people perform afterwards?
- When did people last practise the unusual case rather than the standard one?
- What would a realistic test reveal that our current training would not?

## Common Misuse

Realism is expensive, sometimes dangerous, and not always the best way to start:
beginners often learn better from simplified conditions, with difficulty added as they
improve. Training that is too narrowly matched to one expected environment can leave
people unprepared for a different one, so variety matters as well as realism. And
surface realism is not the point: an impressive simulator can look exactly right while
missing the cues that matter. Reproduce the difficulties that matter, not the scenery.
