## Principle

Build the smallest thing that tests the assumption you are least sure of, and get a real
answer from real behaviour.

## Explanation

An MVP is defined by the question, not by the size. Its purpose is to resolve the
assumption whose failure would waste the most effort — usually whether anyone wants
this, sometimes whether it can be built, occasionally whether it can be sold at a viable
price. Which one you are testing determines what to build, and the answer differs wildly.

Two failure modes bracket it. Too small, and the result is uninterpretable: nobody used
it, and you cannot tell whether that was the idea, the execution or the audience.
Too large, and it is not an experiment, it is the product with the interesting parts
removed.

"Viable" is the word that does the work. The thing must be good enough that using it
tells you something true. A deliberately poor version answers a question you did not
ask.

## Military Example

Limited fielding of new equipment to one unit before a full procurement is the same
logic: the trial must be representative enough that the findings transfer, and small
enough that a poor result is recoverable. A trial conducted under unrealistically
favourable conditions produces a confident answer to a question nobody needed resolved.

## Leadership Example

Before restructuring an entire function, a leader runs the new arrangement with one team
for a quarter. The point is not to prove it works — one team proves little — but to
surface the specific problems that only appear in execution: which decisions become
ambiguous, which handoffs break, what the reporting actually looks like. Those are
cheaper to find once than five times.

## Product Example

A team wanting to test demand for an integration builds a landing page describing it and
counts requests. That tests interest, which is the cheapest question and the weakest
evidence. Testing whether people will use it requires a working version for a few
customers. Testing whether it retains requires months. Each is a different MVP, and
confusing them produces confident conclusions from the wrong experiment.

## Questions

- Which assumption, if wrong, would waste the most effort?
- What is the smallest thing that gives a real answer to that specific question?
- If nobody uses it, will I be able to tell why?
- Am I building an experiment or the product with pieces missing?

## Common Misuse

The term is routinely used to justify shipping something poor, with "minimum" doing all
the work and "viable" ignored — which damages the brand and produces uninterpretable
results. It is equally used as a label for a first release that tests nothing and was
always going to be built. And some things cannot be MVP'd: safety-critical systems,
regulated products, and anything where a bad first impression cannot be recovered.
