## Principle

When the future is uncertain, the right to decide later — without the obligation to —
can be worth paying for.

## Explanation

Most plans are built around the most likely forecast. Optionality asks a different
question: which choices keep good paths open if the forecast is wrong? A shorter lease
with an expansion right, a pilot before a programme, a design that lets a component be
replaced — each costs more than the fully committed version and buys the ability to act
once uncertainty has narrowed.

Finance supplies the discipline, through the idea of "real options". An option has three
parts, and an arrangement that lacks any of them is not really one: a price paid now, a
decision that stays open, and a trigger or expiry date when that decision will be taken.
Options are worth most when uncertainty is high, when it will genuinely resolve with
time, and when the eventual decision has a large upside or downside. They are worth
little when waiting teaches nothing, or when the window is closing anyway.

The hidden costs are real. Options consume attention as well as money, and a portfolio
of unexercised options can quietly become a strategy of never deciding. The test of a
good option is that its holder knows what result would cause them to exercise it, and
what would cause them to let it lapse.

## Military Example

A force establishing a temporary base keeps two feasible resupply routes rather than
optimising entirely for the shortest. The second route costs reconnaissance, engineering
work and a standing plan, but if weather or enemy action closes the first, movement
continues. The commander also sets the trigger in advance: if the primary route is
closed for more than a set period, the alternative becomes the main route.

## Leadership Example

A company needs more capacity, but demand is uncertain. Instead of a ten-year lease, it
negotiates a three-year lease with a right of first refusal on the adjacent floor. The
premium is explicit. So is the trigger: if utilisation stays above a threshold for two
quarters, it exercises the right; if not, it lets it lapse without treating that as a
failure.

## Product Example

Before committing a roadmap to a partner integration, a team builds a narrow prototype
with one customer and a defined success measure. The prototype costs six weeks. It
leaves the team free to expand, rework or drop the integration, and it ends on a fixed date, at which
point the team makes the decision it had deferred.

## Questions

- Which decision can usefully wait, and what will we learn by waiting?
- What does keeping this option open cost, including attention?
- What signal or date ends the option, and what will we do then?
- Is this a real option, or a way of avoiding a decision?

## Common Misuse

Optionality becomes a flattering name for indecision. Keeping every path open destroys
focus, and to colleagues and partners it reads as a lack of commitment, which has its
own cost — some outcomes need a credible, irreversible commitment precisely because it
removes your options. Options also mislead when their price is understated: the cheap
pilot that absorbs the best people is not cheap. Buy options deliberately, give each a
trigger and an expiry, and commit when the evidence arrives.
