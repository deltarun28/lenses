## Principle

Certainty does not arrive before the decision is needed. Build plans that work on partial
information.

## Explanation

The instinct under uncertainty is to gather more. Sometimes correct — often the
information does not exist yet, arrives after the moment has passed, or is contradicted
by the next report. Waiting is itself a decision, and usually the worst available one.

The practical response is not better information but different plans. Decide what
minimum you need to act, identify which unknowns would actually change your choice, and
ignore the rest. Prefer options that remain viable under several versions of reality
over options optimised for one.

Fog also affects your own side. The picture of where your people are and what they are
doing is a reconstruction with delay in it — and it is usually treated as more reliable
than the picture of the opposition, which is a mistake.

## Military Example

Reports arrive late, partial and mutually contradictory. A commander who waits for a
coherent picture cedes tempo to whoever acts on an incomplete one. The discipline is to
identify the one or two facts that would change the plan, task collection specifically
at those, and treat the remaining uncertainty as a condition rather than a problem to be
solved.

## Leadership Example

An executive facing an unexpected competitor move commissions analysis. Six weeks later
the analysis is thorough, the market has moved, and the options that existed at the
start have closed. The better question at the outset was narrower: which single unknown
would change what we do, and what is the fastest way to resolve just that?

## Product Example

A team debates a design decision that depends on how customers will behave, which nobody
knows. The resolvable version is smaller: build the cheapest thing that reveals the
behaviour, and keep the decision reversible until it does. Teams that wait for
certainty in product decisions wait indefinitely, because the information is generated
by shipping.

## Questions

- What is the minimum I need to know to act?
- Which unknowns would actually change my decision, and which am I just uncomfortable about?
- Is this option robust across several versions of what might be true?
- How current is my picture of my own side?

## Common Misuse

It becomes an excuse for not gathering information that was cheaply available, and for
presenting impulsiveness as decisiveness. The claim is that certainty is unattainable in
time, not that preparation is pointless — reconnaissance, research and testing all
reduce fog materially. It also does not license ignoring the information you do have
because it is inconvenient; that is not fog, that is a decision to look away.
