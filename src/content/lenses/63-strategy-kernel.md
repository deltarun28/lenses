## Principle

A strategy has three parts: a diagnosis of the critical challenge, a guiding policy for
dealing with it, and coherent actions that carry the policy out.

## Explanation

The strategy scholar Richard Rumelt called these three parts the kernel of a strategy,
and his argument was mainly about what strategy is not. A goal ("grow 20 per cent"), a
vision, or a long list of priorities is not a strategy, because none of them says what
is in the way or how it will be overcome.

The diagnosis does the most work and is most often skipped. It names the obstacle that
matters most — not every problem, and not the most visible symptom. A good diagnosis
simplifies: it makes some facts central and others irrelevant.

The guiding policy is the overall approach to that obstacle. Its test is whether it
rules things out. A policy that would be consistent with every proposed action is a
slogan.

The actions must be coherent: they support one another and concentrate resources on the
diagnosed challenge, rather than spreading them across every worthwhile activity.
Coherence is what makes a strategy stronger than the sum of its parts, and it is why a
real strategy always produces some disappointed people whose projects did not fit.

Rumelt's framework is an argument drawn from cases, not a tested model, but it gives a
quick, demanding check on any document called a strategy.

## Military Example

A commander diagnoses that the operation's real limit is not combat power but
sustainment: supply lines are long and exposed. The guiding policy is to advance only as
fast as supply can follow. The actions fit together: securing the main supply route,
establishing forward stocks before each phase, and holding back units that would outrun
their fuel. A request for additional armour is declined, because it would worsen the
diagnosed problem.

## Leadership Example

A charity's plan lists fourteen priorities. Asked for the diagnosis, the leadership
agrees on one: donors leave after their first gift because they hear nothing about what
it achieved. The guiding policy is to make every first-time donor see an outcome within
ninety days. Eleven of the fourteen priorities are deferred; the remaining three, and
two new actions, all serve that policy.

## Product Example

A product team diagnoses that new users leave before they reach their first successful
outcome. Its policy is to shorten the time to that outcome. The actions remove optional
setup, pre-fill example data and measure time to first success. Proposals for advanced
features are deferred, because they deepen the product for people who never reach it.

## Questions

- What is the single most important obstacle, as distinct from its symptoms?
- What does our guiding policy rule out?
- Do our actions reinforce one another, or merely coexist?
- Whose project does this strategy disappoint?

## Common Misuse

The kernel is reduced to three sentences of branding, written after the plan was already
decided. A diagnosis can be wrong, and a framework that rewards confident simplification
can make a wrong one hard to question; it has to be revisited as evidence arrives. Large
organisations may also need several linked strategies rather than one. The value is in
concentration and honest diagnosis, not in fitting every activity under one heading.
