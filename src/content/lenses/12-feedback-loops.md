## Principle

Every system contains loops that amplify change and loops that resist it. Behaviour
over time is the result of which dominates.

## Explanation

A reinforcing loop feeds output back as input: more produces more, and the result is
exponential growth or collapse. A balancing loop pushes back towards a target: the
further from it you get, the harder the correction. Most systems contain both, and what
you observe is whichever currently dominates.

The complication is delay. When feedback arrives late, people overcorrect — the
response is still in transit when the next adjustment is made, producing the
oscillation seen in hiring cycles, inventory swings and inflation policy.

The practical question is not "what is happening" but "which loop is dominant, and what
delay is in it". That determines whether to push harder, stop pushing, or wait.

## Military Example

Escalation is a reinforcing loop: each side responds to the other's last action, and
the response becomes the next stimulus. Rules of engagement, chains of approval and
established communication channels exist as balancing loops deliberately inserted into
it. Removing them for speed removes the thing preventing a local incident from
compounding into something neither side chose.

## Leadership Example

An organisation with a reputation for developing people attracts stronger applicants,
who make it better at developing people. That is reinforcing, and it compounds quietly
for years. It runs equally well in reverse: departures increase load on those
remaining, which causes further departures. The point to intervene is early, because
the loop's strength is what makes it hard to reverse later.

## Product Example

A marketplace where more sellers attract more buyers who attract more sellers is
reinforcing, and it is why early liquidity matters more than features. Meanwhile
support load acts as a balancing loop on growth: past a point, every new customer
degrades the experience of existing ones. Teams that see only the reinforcing loop keep
pushing acquisition into a system that is actively resisting.

## Questions

- Is the dominant loop here amplifying the change or resisting it?
- How long is the delay between an action and its feedback?
- Am I overcorrecting because the last correction has not arrived yet?
- If this is reinforcing, what eventually stops it — and do I want to be there when it does?

## Common Misuse

Loop diagrams are easy to draw and easy to mistake for analysis; the useful output is
identifying one or two leverage points, not a complete map. There is also a tendency to
see reinforcing loops everywhere, when most systems are dominated by balancing loops
that quietly return things to where they were — which is why so many interventions
produce a brief improvement and no lasting change.
