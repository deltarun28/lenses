## Principle

Cost is not what something takes. It is what it prevents.

## Explanation

Money is the visible cost and usually the least important one. The binding constraints
in most organisations are attention, calendar time, and the small number of people who
can do difficult work. Spend those on one thing and they are unavailable for another,
regardless of budget.

The discipline is comparative. "Is this worth doing?" is nearly always answered yes,
because almost anything has some positive return. The useful question is "is this the
best available use of the resource it consumes?" — which forces a named alternative.

This is also why long lists of priorities are a symptom rather than a plan. If
everything is a priority, no opportunity cost has been acknowledged, and the choice
gets made later by whoever is loudest.

## Military Example

A commander commits the reserve to reinforce success on one axis. The cost is not the
sub-unit's fuel and ammunition; it is the loss of the ability to respond to anything
unexpected for the rest of the operation. That is why committing the reserve is treated
as one of the few genuinely irreversible decisions, and why re-establishing one is an
immediate priority afterwards.

## Leadership Example

An executive agrees to sponsor a working group. The cost is four hours a month on the
calendar and, more significantly, the attention that would have gone to the two
decisions only they can make. A year of small, individually reasonable commitments
produces a leader who is fully occupied and no longer doing the work that requires their
authority.

## Product Example

A team spends a quarter building a requested integration for a large customer. The
integration is valuable and the customer is real. The cost is the payments work that
did not happen, which would have removed friction for every customer. Both were worth
doing; only one could be done, and the choice was made by whoever asked most
insistently rather than on the comparison.

## Questions

- What is the single best thing this resource could otherwise do?
- What am I deciding not to do by saying yes to this?
- Which constraint does this actually consume — money, calendar time, or scarce people?
- If this were the only thing we did this quarter, would it be the right choice?

## Common Misuse

Applied to every small decision it becomes paralysis, and the analysis costs more than
the choice is worth. It is also frequently used to justify doing nothing, since some
hypothetical better use can always be imagined — but an unnamed alternative is not an
alternative. The comparison must be against a real, available option, and past spending
does not enter into it: sunk costs are gone regardless of which path you take now.
