## Principle

Spend decision effort in proportion to the cost of being wrong and unable to undo it.

## Explanation

Organisations tend to apply one decision process to everything. Applied to reversible
choices, a careful process is slow and expensive; applied to irreversible ones, a fast
process is reckless. Jeff Bezos described the distinction in a 2015 letter to
shareholders as one-way and two-way doors: a two-way door can be walked back through if
the room is wrong, so the decision should be made quickly by a small group; a one-way
door cannot, so it deserves deliberation.

The value is not only speed. For a reversible decision, acting is often the best
research available, because the consequences reveal what analysis could only guess.
Delay costs more than a mistake that can be corrected.

The difficulty is that reversibility is rarely labelled. It has to be assessed in
practice: what would undoing this actually involve? Data that cannot be migrated back,
contracts with exit penalties, public announcements, people hired or let go, and habits
customers have formed all make an apparently easy choice sticky. The opposite also
holds: many one-way decisions can be partly converted into two-way ones by staging them,
testing a part first, or keeping an exit open.

## Military Example

Committing a reserve is close to irreversible within the time of a battle: once it is
engaged, it cannot be quickly recovered for a different task. Choosing the route for a
reconnaissance patrol is not. A commander who deliberates over patrol routes while
committing the reserve on a first report has the effort the wrong way round. Doctrine
reflects this by tying the reserve decision to specific conditions set out in advance.

## Leadership Example

Trialling a new weekly meeting format is reversible; the leader announces it as a
four-week experiment and reviews it. Closing a regional office is not: the people who
leave do not return, and the local relationships go with them. The same leader runs the
closure through scenario analysis, consultation and a check of what would have to be
true for it to be wrong.

## Product Example

A team tests a redesigned flow behind a feature flag that can be switched off in
seconds, and ships it the same week. A database schema change that discards a field is
treated differently: a rehearsal on a copy of production data, a backup tested by
actually restoring it, and an explicit sign-off.

## Questions

- If this turns out to be wrong, what would undoing it actually involve?
- Where is the hidden lock-in — data, contracts, reputation, habits?
- Can part of this be tried first, so the irreversible step comes later?
- Are we slowing down a reversible decision out of habit?

## Common Misuse

Reversibility becomes an excuse for careless decisions, on the grounds that anything can
be undone. Undoing is rarely free: each reversal costs time and credibility, and people
stop taking the decision seriously. Many small reversible decisions can also add up to
an irreversible pattern — a codebase, a culture, a brand. And some one-way decisions
cannot wait for certainty; slowing down is itself a choice with consequences. Use the
distinction to allocate care, not to avoid it.
