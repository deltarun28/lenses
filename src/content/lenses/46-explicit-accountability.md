## Principle

For every outcome that matters, name one person who answers for it, before it is needed.

## Explanation

When several people share a responsibility, each tends to do less. In John Darley and
Bibb Latané's 1968 experiments, people who believed others had also heard an apparent
emergency were much slower and less likely to help than people who believed they were
alone. Related research on "social loafing" found that people put in less effort in
groups when their individual contribution cannot be identified. These are laboratory
findings, and real organisations add hierarchy, incentives and relationships, but the
pattern is familiar to anyone who has watched an important email go unanswered because
it was sent to twelve people.

Accountability, in the sense meant here, is answerability: being the person who must
give an account of the outcome, who decides trade-offs when they arise, and who is
expected to notice when it is going wrong. It is different from doing the work, which
many people can share, and different from blame, which is assigned after a failure.
Explicit means three things: named in advance, known to everyone involved, and accepted
by the person named.

The gaps appear at the seams. Outcomes that cross team boundaries — a handoff, a
customer's experience from end to end, a shared system, a risk that touches every
department — are where each team can do its part correctly and the outcome still fails.
Committees create the same gap: a committee can advise, but someone has to own what it
decides.

This is distinct from the next question, whether that person has the power to deliver.
Authority Must Match Accountability deals with that.

## Military Example

The boundary between two adjacent units is a classic weak point, because each can assume
the other is watching it. Orders therefore assign responsibility for coordinating along
a boundary to one of the two units by name. The assignment does not make the other unit
irrelevant; it means that if a gap opens, one commander knows it is theirs to close.

## Leadership Example

A customer's complaint about a billing error passes between sales, finance and support.
Each team handles its part correctly and passes it on. The customer waits six weeks. The
company appoints a named owner for every complaint, responsible for the outcome — the
customer's problem resolved — with authority to call on the other teams. Resolution
times fall sharply without any team working harder.

## Product Example

A shared software component is "owned by everyone" because every team uses it. Security
updates to it lag by months, since each team assumes another will apply them. Ownership
is assigned to one team on a rotating basis, with the update backlog visible to all. The
lag disappears within a quarter.

## Questions

- Who, by name, answers for this outcome?
- Would everyone involved give the same name?
- Which outcomes cross our team boundaries and belong to nobody?
- Does the named person know, and accept, what they are accountable for?

## Common Misuse

A named owner can become a designated scapegoat; accountability assigned only after a
failure is blame by another name. Naming one owner can also give everyone else
permission to disengage — "not my job" — when the outcome still needs their
contribution. Single-point accountability can overload a few capable people with
everything that crosses a boundary. And some outcomes, such as safety and conduct, need
everyone to feel responsible as well as one person to answer for them.
