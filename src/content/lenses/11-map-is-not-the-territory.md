## Principle

A representation is useful because it discards detail. Remember what it discarded.

## Explanation

Dashboards, org charts, plans, forecasts and status reports are all maps. They are
valuable precisely because they simplify — a representation that included everything
would be as unusable as the thing itself.

The danger is that maps are what leaders actually interact with. The further you are
from the work, the more your view of reality consists of summaries produced by other
people, each of which involved decisions about what to leave out. Over time the map
becomes the object of management, and the territory drifts away from it unobserved.

The correction is cheap and unpopular: periodic direct contact with the thing itself.
Not to replace the map, but to recalibrate it and to find out what it stopped showing.

## Military Example

A situation map shows unit positions, boundaries and reported strengths. It does not
show that one company has been in contact for eighteen hours, that the ground between
two positions is impassable to vehicles after rain, or that a subordinate's confidence
in a route is based on a single reconnaissance. Commanders visit forward positions not
for morale but for calibration — to discover which parts of the picture are current and
which are inherited.

## Leadership Example

An engagement survey reports 78 per cent favourable. The number is real and useless on
its own: it aggregates a team in crisis with three that are fine, and the question
wording rewards politeness. Leaders who manage the score optimise the survey. Leaders
who treat it as an indicator go and find out which team produced the outlying answers
and why.

## Product Example

Analytics show a funnel with a 4 per cent drop at one step, which looks minor. Watching
ten session recordings reveals that the users who drop are the ones who read the
pricing detail carefully — the segment most likely to buy. The metric was accurate; it
simply did not encode who was leaving, and that was the entire finding.

## Questions

- What does this representation deliberately leave out?
- When did I last look at the thing itself rather than a summary of it?
- Who produced this map, and what were they optimising when they made it?
- Which decision am I about to make that depends on the discarded detail?

## Common Misuse

The phrase is used to dismiss data that is inconvenient — "the numbers don't tell the
whole story" is true and is also the standard defence of unaccountable judgement. Maps
are usually more reliable than an individual's impressions, particularly at scale. The
failure being described is not measurement; it is mistaking the measurement for the
thing, and never checking the gap between them.
