## Principle

A shared vocabulary and shared standards of reasoning are infrastructure: they make
coordination cheap, and are noticed mainly when they are missing.

## Explanation

When two people trained in the same discipline work together, much goes unsaid. They
know what a term means, what counts as evidence, what a complete argument looks like and
what a standard report contains. Military doctrine, clinical conventions in medicine,
legal reasoning and engineering standards all work this way. They let people predict how
others will reason and act, so less has to be negotiated and fewer things go wrong in
translation.

Research on teams supports the intuition. Teams whose members share an understanding of
the task and of each other's roles — what researchers call shared mental models — tend
to perform better, according to a meta-analysis by Leslie DeChurch and Jessica
Mesmer-Magnus. One consequence is easy to overlook: education produces compatibility as
well as capability. People who learned the same frameworks can work together quickly,
even as strangers.

Like other infrastructure, shared frameworks are built slowly, need maintenance, and
decay without anyone noticing. Mergers, rapid growth, heavy use of contractors and
cross-disciplinary teams expose their absence, often through words that everyone uses
and nobody defines in the same way: "done", "risk", "forecast", "customer".

They also have a cost, which the lens on Correlated Errors examines: people who
think alike are efficient together and share the same blind spots. The healthiest form
of shared framework is agreement on how to reason, argue and test claims, rather than
agreement on the conclusions.

## Military Example

Units from different nations can operate together largely because they share doctrine: a
standard format for orders, common terms and agreed symbols. Before one operation, a
liaison officer notices that the word "secure" means "occupy and hold" to one contingent
and "check and move through" to another. The difference is resolved in a meeting;
discovered during the operation, it would have left a key position unoccupied.

## Leadership Example

After a merger, two finance teams both produce "forecasts". One means a target the
business is committed to reach; the other means its best estimate of what will happen.
Board papers contradict each other for months. A short shared glossary, and an agreed
standard for what a forecast must contain, removes a recurring source of confusion and
argument.

## Product Example

A growing engineering organisation adopts a common format for design proposals: the
problem, the options considered, the trade-offs and the decision. Reviews speed up,
because everyone knows where to look and what counts as a complete argument, and
newcomers learn the organisation's standards of reasoning by reading past proposals.

## Questions

- Which important terms mean different things to different groups here?
- What must a newcomer learn before they can argue effectively here?
- Do we pass on our frameworks deliberately, or leave people to absorb them?
- Do we share standards of reasoning, or only conclusions?

## Common Misuse

Shared frameworks turn into jargon that excludes outsiders and becomes a test of
membership rather than a tool. They harden, so that the framework survives long after
the problems it fitted have changed. Imposing one framework on disciplines with good
reasons to think differently destroys useful variety. And shared words can hide
disagreement: people who use the same term confidently may not mean the same thing.
Standardise what reduces friction, and leave room for different ways of thinking.
