## Principle

The gap between a plan and its execution is filled by countless small difficulties, none
individually significant.

## Explanation

Friction is not any single obstacle. It is weather, fatigue, a misheard instruction,
equipment that works differently than expected, a person who is ill, a document that
took two days to approve. Each is trivial and none appears in the plan. Together they
are why simple things are hard.

The important consequence is compounding. Complexity multiplies friction, because each
dependency is another surface for small failures to accumulate on, and each handoff adds
delay to every subsequent step. This is the real argument for simple plans — not that
simple plans are more elegant, but that complicated ones have more places to grind.

The two defences are slack and simplicity. Slack absorbs friction without transmitting
it forward. Simplicity reduces how much there is to absorb.

## Military Example

A move planned for four hours takes seven. No single cause: a vehicle that would not
start, a route confirmation that took forty minutes, a section that had not eaten. The
plan was not wrong; it assumed the absence of friction. This is why timings carry
margin, why orders are kept simple enough to be executed by tired people, and why
rehearsals matter more than briefings.

## Leadership Example

A change programme with eleven dependencies across five departments is planned to a
timeline where each step follows the last. Every step encounters a small delay — an
approval, an absence, a misunderstanding — and because each is sequential, the delays
add rather than overlap. The programme is six weeks late without anything having gone
identifiably wrong.

## Product Example

A two-day task takes a week: the environment was broken, a dependency needed upgrading,
review took three days because the reviewer was in workshops, and the deploy window was
missed. Estimates fail not because the work was misjudged but because estimates price
the work and not the friction around it. Teams that track actuals discover their
multiplier is remarkably stable.

## Questions

- What is my plan assuming will go smoothly?
- How many handoffs and dependencies does this have, and what does each add?
- Is there slack anywhere, or does every step start when the last one was meant to finish?
- Could this be executed by someone tired, on a bad day, with the wrong equipment?

## Common Misuse

Friction becomes a universal excuse — an unfalsifiable reason for any overrun, which
prevents anyone examining causes that were specific and avoidable. It also gets used to
justify padding rather than simplification, when reducing dependencies usually does more
than adding time. And the concept does not mean plans are pointless: planning is what
identifies which friction matters, even when the plan itself does not survive.
