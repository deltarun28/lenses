## Principle

Most damaging behaviour comes from constraint and confusion, not from hostile intent.

## Explanation

When someone's action harms you, the mind supplies a motive. Motive is a compact,
satisfying explanation, and it is usually wrong — most people are not organising
against you, they are busy, under-informed, badly incentivised, or working from
different facts.

The reason this matters is not charity. It is accuracy of response. If you misdiagnose
constraint as malice, your response escalates a solvable problem: you go around the
person, harden your position, and create the adversary you assumed you had. If you
misdiagnose malice as constraint, you lose some time and learn quickly.

Note the wording: _adequately_ explained. This is a default, not a verdict. Repeated
behaviour after clear feedback is evidence, and the razor stops applying.

## Military Example

A supporting unit fails to arrive at the agreed line of departure and a commander reads
it as a deliberate reallocation of their resources. The actual cause is a route change
issued after the orders group, which never reached them. The commander who assumes
intent redistributes assets and damages a working relationship. The one who checks
first fixes a distribution problem in the orders process — a fault that would otherwise
recur.

## Leadership Example

A department head repeatedly bypasses another team's process and is labelled
territorial. In conversation it emerges they were told the process added six weeks and
never checked whether that was still true. The behaviour is real and needs to change,
but the fix — correct the belief, agree an escalation path — is different from the fix
for someone protecting their patch.

## Product Example

An engineering team ships a change that breaks a workflow the support team relies on.
Support reads it as engineering not caring about their work. The cause is that the
workflow was undocumented and invisible in the codebase. Treating it as indifference
produces a complaint. Treating it as a visibility gap produces a test that fails next
time.

## Questions

- What pressure or missing information would make this behaviour reasonable from their side?
- What do they know or believe that I do not?
- Has this happened before after clear feedback — or is this the first instance?
- Would I still hold this interpretation if I liked this person?

## Common Misuse

The razor becomes a way to excuse repeated harm and avoid confrontation: assuming good
faith indefinitely lets genuinely bad behaviour continue unaddressed, and colleagues
notice. Incompetence also has consequences whether or not it was intended, so the
explanation does not remove the need for a response. It changes what the response
should be. And in adversarial settings — competitors, negotiations, actual opponents —
hostile intent is the correct default, not the exception.
