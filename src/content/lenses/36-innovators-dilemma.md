## Principle

Established organisations lose to inferior entrants because listening to their best
customers rationally prevents them from responding.

## Explanation

The trap is that every individual decision is correct. Your most valuable customers want
improvements to the existing product. The new entrant serves a segment you do not want,
at margins you cannot justify, with something measurably worse on every axis your
customers care about.

Declining that market is the right call by any current measure. Meanwhile the entrant
improves along a different trajectory, and when it becomes adequate for the mainstream,
the incumbent's cost structure, sales channel and internal metrics are all built around
the old market.

What makes it a dilemma rather than a mistake is that the correct response — investing
in a market that is smaller, worse and less profitable — cannot be justified using the
processes that made the organisation successful.

## Military Example

An organisation with expensive, capable platforms and the training pipeline to match
faces cheap attritable systems that are inferior on every traditional measure. The
sensible investment is the platform that wins today's assessment. The cheap system
improves on a different curve, and by the time it is adequate the incumbent's
infrastructure, career structures and doctrine are all built around the other answer.

## Leadership Example

A services business declines small clients because they consume the same overhead at a
fraction of the fee. A competitor serves them with a lighter, standardised process,
learns to do it profitably, and moves upmarket carrying a cost structure the incumbent
cannot match. The original decision was correct on every account the incumbent looked
at.

## Product Example

A team maintaining a comprehensive enterprise product hears consistently from its
largest accounts that they want depth. A simpler competitor with a fraction of the
functionality is adopted by individuals and small teams, becomes adequate for
departments, and arrives in the enterprise from the bottom — bought by users rather than
by procurement, which is the channel the incumbent does not have.

## Questions

- Who is serving the customers we have decided are not worth having?
- Is something currently worse than us improving faster than us?
- Which of our processes would prevent us from pursuing a smaller, lower-margin market?
- Are we measuring the entrant on our axis or on theirs?

## Common Misuse

"Disruption" has become a label for any competitive loss, including ordinary cases of
being beaten by a better product — which is not this pattern at all. The framework is
also used to justify pursuing every low-end entrant, when most genuinely are worse and
stay worse, and chasing them all is ruinous. The discriminating question is whether the
entrant's improvement trajectory is steeper than the mainstream's requirement, and that
is only ever clear in retrospect.
