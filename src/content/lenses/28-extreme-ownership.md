## Principle

When something in your area fails, look first for the cause you controlled.

## Explanation

There is always a true external explanation available: the brief was unclear, the
person underperformed, the market moved. These explanations are frequently accurate and
completely useless, because none of them is a lever you can pull.

The discipline is to search for your own contribution first — what you failed to
specify, verify, resource or notice — not as self-criticism but because that is the
only part of the causal chain you can change next time.

It is a stance for the person accountable, not a theory of causation. The failure was
genuinely multi-causal. You are choosing to work on your share because it is the share
that responds to effort.

## Military Example

After a failed action, a commander examining their own orders, rehearsals and
allocation of resources will find things to fix. A commander who concludes that a
subordinate underperformed has nothing to work with, and the same failure recurs with a
different subordinate. This is why after action reviews start with the plan rather than
with the people executing it.

## Leadership Example

A project misses its date because a dependency was late. The external account is
accurate. The version that changes anything: the dependency was never confirmed in
writing, no check was scheduled, and the first signal of slippage arrived a week after
it was known to the other team. All three were within the project lead's control.

## Product Example

A launch underperforms and the team concludes that marketing did not support it. Also
possibly true. What the team controlled: nobody defined what success looked like before
the build, no one talked to marketing until the fortnight before launch, and the feature
shipped without the instrumentation needed to tell whether it worked. Those are the
findings that make the next launch different.

## Questions

- What did I control that contributed to this?
- What did I assume without verifying?
- What would I need to change to prevent this happening again, that does not depend on someone else changing?
- Am I reaching for an accurate explanation because it is comfortable?

## Common Misuse

The idea gets weaponised downwards — leaders demanding ownership from their teams while
attributing their own failures outward, which is the exact inversion of it. Taken too
literally it also becomes destructive: claiming responsibility for genuinely external
events produces burnout and obscures real causes, and some failures really are somebody
else's. It is a search strategy for actionable causes, not a claim that everything is
your fault.
