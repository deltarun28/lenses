## Principle

Allocate work by what each person gives up by doing it, not by who is best at it.

## Explanation

The economist David Ricardo showed in 1817 that two countries both gain from trade even
when one is better at producing everything, provided each specialises in what it gives
up least to produce. The same logic applies to people, teams and organisations, and it
overturns a common instinct.

The classic illustration is a lawyer who types faster than their assistant. The lawyer
should still not do the typing, because every hour spent typing is an hour not spent on
legal work worth far more. Absolute advantage — being best — says the lawyer should
type. Comparative advantage — the lowest opportunity cost — says they should not.

In organisations the instinct to give work to the most capable person creates a
recognisable pattern: the expert who is best at everything becomes the bottleneck for
everything, does a great deal of work that others could do adequately, and has no time
for the work only they can do. The total output of the team is lower than if tasks had
been allocated by opportunity cost.

The calculation includes more than speed. Quality requirements, the cost of training
someone, coordination overhead and risk all count. Sometimes the expert should do the
work because "adequately" is not good enough or the handover costs more than it saves.
The point is to make that judgement deliberately, not by default.

## Military Example

A unit's most experienced technician can repair every fault fastest, and is also the
only person able to diagnose a recurring system failure. While the technician handles
routine repairs, the recurring fault keeps generating more of them. The unit trains two
junior technicians on routine work, freeing the expert to find the root cause and remove
the source of the demand.

## Leadership Example

A senior manager writes every client update, because the manager writes them best.
Meanwhile, a contract renegotiation that only the manager can lead slips by a month. The
manager drafts a template, reviews a colleague's first few updates closely, then only
samples them. The updates are slightly less polished; the renegotiation happens on time.

## Product Example

A startup's lead engineer reviews every pull request because their reviews catch the
most problems. Review becomes the team's constraint, and the engineer has stopped
working on the architectural decisions nobody else can make. The team splits review by
risk: routine changes are reviewed by peers, and the lead reviews only changes to core
systems.

## Questions

- Am I doing this because I am best at it, or because my time is cheapest here?
- What is not getting done while I do this?
- Who could do this adequately, with some support?
- What can only I do, and is it getting my time?

## Common Misuse

Comparative advantage is invoked to offload work without the training, context or
authority needed to do it well, and the result is rework that costs more than the time
saved. Allocations also harden: people stay in the roles their early advantages
suggested and never develop others, so yesterday's sensible specialisation becomes
tomorrow's single point of failure. Some tasks — judgement calls, sensitive
conversations — need the best person regardless. Revisit allocations as people grow.
