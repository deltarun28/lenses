## Principle

Every decision has consequences, and those consequences have consequences. Judge the
decision on the second set.

## Explanation

First-order thinking stops at the immediate effect: we do X, and X happens. It is
adequate in static situations and misleading everywhere else, because most environments
contain people and systems that respond.

Second-order thinking continues the sequence. We do X, X happens, and then people
adapt to X — which may reverse the original benefit. The question is always "and then
what?", asked at least twice.

You cannot forecast every downstream effect, and trying to produces false precision.
What is achievable is identifying who has an incentive to respond, and what their
cheapest response is. That single step catches most of the damage.

## Military Example

A checkpoint on the main route reduces the movement of contraband along it. That is the
first-order effect and it is real. The second-order effect is that movement shifts to
secondary routes through populated areas, where interdiction is harder and the risk to
civilians is higher. The checkpoint may still be correct, but its value must be judged
against the displacement it causes, not against the traffic it stops.

## Leadership Example

A leader introduces approval for any spend above a low threshold to control costs.
Spending falls. Then requests are split into amounts just below the threshold,
decisions that used to take a day take two weeks, and managers stop proposing small
experiments because the approval cost exceeds the value. The control worked; the
organisation got slower and less honest.

## Product Example

A team removes a confusing settings page to simplify onboarding, and completion rates
rise. Three months later, support volume increases: the small group who needed those
settings now contact support instead, and each contact costs far more than the page
did. The first-order metric improved while total cost went up.

## Questions

- And then what happens? Ask it twice more.
- Who is affected by this, and what is their cheapest available response?
- Which of the benefits depend on nobody adapting?
- What does this look like in a year if everyone behaves rationally?

## Common Misuse

Second-order thinking is easily turned into an excuse for inaction — any proposal can
be defeated by inventing enough downstream harms, and cautious organisations use it
that way. It also invites false precision: third- and fourth-order forecasts in complex
systems are mostly speculation dressed as rigour. Go one or two steps beyond the
obvious, focus on responses that have an incentive behind them, and then decide.
