## Principle

Once you hold a belief, you gather evidence for it rather than about it.

## Explanation

The bias operates at three stages, and the first is the most damaging. You choose which
questions to ask and where to look, and those choices are shaped by the answer you
expect. Then you weigh confirming evidence as solid and disconfirming evidence as
flawed. Then you remember the hits.

Because the first stage is about search, more effort makes it worse rather than better.
An analyst who spends a week gathering support for a hypothesis ends up more confident
and no more accurate.

The only reliable countermeasure is structural: specify in advance what evidence would
change your mind, and assign someone the job of finding it. Willpower does not work
here, because the bias operates before you notice you are reasoning.

## Military Example

An intelligence picture forms early that an adversary intends to attack on one axis.
Subsequent collection is tasked to that axis, so indicators there accumulate and
indicators elsewhere are never sought. The picture becomes more detailed and more
confident without becoming more accurate. Formal red teaming exists precisely because
the failure is in the tasking, not in the analysts.

## Leadership Example

A leader decides a senior hire is not working out. From that point, missed details
register as evidence and good work reads as the minimum expected. Colleagues asked for
input hear the framing in the question and supply matching examples. The judgement may
well be right — but nothing in the process after the initial impression is capable of
showing that it is wrong.

## Product Example

A team convinced of a feature runs interviews that describe it enthusiastically and
ask whether users would find it useful. Everyone says yes. The interviews that would
have been informative — asking what people did last time they had the problem, without
mentioning the solution — were never run, because the goal was validation rather than
discovery.

## Questions

- What evidence would change my mind, and have I gone looking for it?
- Am I asking a question, or looking for support for an answer?
- Who disagrees with this, and have I heard their strongest version?
- If I believed the opposite, where would I look first?

## Common Misuse

The label is mostly deployed against other people, which is itself an instance of the
bias — noticing it in an opponent's reasoning and not your own. It is also used to
dismiss any consistent position, but holding a belief while accumulating supporting
evidence is not automatically an error; sometimes the belief is correct. The
distinguishing question is whether disconfirming evidence was ever genuinely sought.
