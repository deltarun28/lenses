## Principle

Organisations value the work they can see, and the most important work is often the
hardest to see.

## Explanation

Some work leaves visible evidence: a launch, a report, a closed ticket, a late night at
the desk. Other work produces an absence: the outage that did not happen, the dispute
settled before it escalated, the colleague unblocked, the equipment that kept running
because it was serviced. Praise, promotion and budgets follow what can be observed, so
visible work is over-rewarded and invisible work is the first to be cut when money is
short.

It helps to separate three things that are easily confused. Activity is effort that can
be seen: hours, meetings, messages. Output is what gets produced: documents, features,
repairs. Outcome is the change in the world that actually matters: fewer failures,
satisfied customers, a ready force. Activity is easiest to observe and least connected
to value. Outcome is hardest to observe, and for prevention work it is measured as
something that did not happen, which means comparing against what would otherwise have
occurred.

Nelson Repenning and John Sterman described the consequence in a study of improvement
programmes in manufacturing, with the memorable title "Nobody Ever Gets Credit for
Fixing Problems that Never Happened". Managers rewarded the people who rescued failing
processes, so effort shifted from prevention to rescue, which produced more failures to
rescue.

This differs from Goodhart's Law, which is about a measure that degrades once it becomes
a target. The visibility problem comes earlier: it decides which work gets noticed at
all, before anyone has chosen a metric.

## Military Example

One unit's vehicles are almost always available; another's break down often and are
recovered by impressive overnight efforts. The second unit's mechanics receive
commendations. A commander who asks why the first unit's availability is high finds a
maintenance section that never misses scheduled servicing. When the next exercise
demands extra people, the commander protects that servicing time instead of borrowing
from it.

## Leadership Example

A team member spends a third of their time answering questions, reviewing others' work
and resolving disagreements between teams. At review time, they have shipped fewer items
than their peers. Their manager asks each team member whose help they relied on most
this year, and the same name keeps coming up. Their evaluation is changed to include
that contribution.

## Product Example

A security team has had no serious incidents for a year, and a budget review proposes
cutting it. The team presents what the absence consisted of: vulnerabilities fixed
before they were exploited, attacks blocked, recovery drills completed, and the incident
rate at comparable companies. The quiet year is shown to be an outcome rather than an
absence of work.

## Questions

- What work here is valuable precisely because nothing happens?
- Are we measuring activity, output, or outcome?
- Whose departure would hurt most, and does our evaluation reflect it?
- What would happen within six months if this work stopped?

## Common Misuse

"Invisible work" can become an unfalsifiable claim: everyone believes their own
contribution is underrated, and some unseen work really is low value. Making all work
visible is not the answer either, because it generates reporting overhead and rewards
people for displaying effort, which is the original problem in a new form. Ask for
evidence of outcomes, including estimates of what was prevented, and accept that some of
that evidence will be judgement rather than measurement.
