## Principle

Structured comparison of intent against outcome, held soon enough that memory is intact
and separately from any question of blame.

## Explanation

The format is four questions: what was meant to happen, what actually happened, why the
difference, and what changes. Its power is entirely in the sequence — establishing the
facts before discussing causes prevents the conversation collapsing into competing
narratives.

Two conditions make it work. It must be held close to the event, because reconstructed
memory converges on a tidy story that never happened. And it must be genuinely separate
from performance assessment, because a review that determines someone's rating produces
careful, useless testimony.

Reviewing successes matters as much as failures. Success is where you learn least by
default, because nobody investigates why something worked, and a win produced by luck
looks identical to one produced by skill until you examine it.

## Military Example

Reviews are conducted immediately after an exercise serial, on the ground, led by
someone who was not commanding it. Participants describe events in sequence before
anyone offers an interpretation. The output is a small number of specific changes to
procedure or preparation — not a general instruction to do better, which is what a
review conducted a fortnight later by the person being assessed reliably produces.

## Leadership Example

An organisation reviews a failed product launch. The temptation is to open with why it
failed, which starts the defence. Establishing the timeline first — the decision to
commit was made on this date, on this information, before this data existed — usually
shows the decision was reasonable and the information flow was not. That is a fixable
finding; "marketing underdelivered" is not.

## Product Example

A team runs a retrospective after every incident and produces the same three actions
each time, none of which are done. The pattern is itself the finding: the reviews have
become ritual because nothing is tracked to completion. A review with no owner, date and
subsequent check is a discussion, and it is cheaper to stop holding it than to keep
pretending.

## Questions

- What did we expect to happen, stated before we knew the outcome?
- Where exactly did the two diverge, and what did we know at that moment?
- Is this a cause we control, or a description of the weather?
- Who owns each change, and when will we check it happened?

## Common Misuse

The most common failure is running them only after failures, which builds a body of
knowledge about what goes wrong and none about what goes right. The second is the blame
review, dressed in the format but understood by everyone present as an inquiry into
fault — after which the honest accounts stop. The third is volume: fifteen actions from
one review means none get done, and three specific ones would have changed something.
