## Principle

A group can choose a course that few of its members want, because each person mistakes
the others' silence for agreement.

## Explanation

The management writer Jerry Harvey named the idea after a family story: on a hot
afternoon, a family drives fifty miles to Abilene for a poor meal, and on the way home
discovers that none of them had wanted to go. Each had agreed because they believed the
others were keen. The story is a parable, but it describes a well-studied effect that
social psychologists call pluralistic ignorance — people privately reject a view while
wrongly believing that most others hold it.

The mechanism is not cowardice. Each person has a reasonable private doubt and an
equally reasonable reading of the room: nobody else has objected, so presumably the
doubt is unfounded or already dealt with. Every silence becomes evidence for the next
person. The group then acts on a preference that exists only as everyone's guess about
everyone else.

The difference from ordinary disagreement is what makes it costly. A group that is
openly divided knows it is divided. A group in Abilene believes it agrees, and so it
stops looking. That is why exhortations to "speak up" do little. What works is a process
that collects private views before they are shaped by public ones.

## Military Example

At a planning conference, every staff section accepts an ambitious movement timetable.
Afterwards, the logistics, communications and engineer representatives each admit they
thought it was unrealistic, but assumed the others had solved their parts. A round of
independent feasibility ratings, written before discussion, would have shown three
sections with the same doubt before the order was issued.

## Leadership Example

A leadership team approves a large annual offsite. Months later several members say they
privately thought it poor value but assumed the chief executive wanted it, while the
chief executive had assumed the team did. The chair changes the procedure for
discretionary decisions: each member writes a preferred option before the most senior
person speaks.

## Product Example

A product team builds a feature after a persuasive early proposal meets no objection. A
later anonymous poll shows that most of the team had preferred fixing onboarding. The
team adopts a silent first vote on roadmap choices, so the record shows what people
thought before they heard each other.

## Questions

- What do I think, before I take my cue from the room?
- Has anyone actually said they want this, or have they only not objected?
- Have we collected views independently before discussing them?
- Would the outcome change if people answered anonymously?

## Common Misuse

Not every consensus is false, and a group that treats all agreement as suspect never
settles anything. The idea can also be used by a determined minority to reopen a
decision it simply lost. And anonymous polling has costs of its own: it can surface
preferences without the reasoning behind them. Use it where hierarchy or politeness is
likely to be hiding real doubt, especially on decisions that are costly to reverse, then
decide and commit.
