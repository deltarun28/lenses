## Principle

An unusually good or bad result is usually followed by a more ordinary one, with or
without intervention.

## Explanation

Any measured outcome mixes stable ability with variation. When a result is extreme, it
is likely that the variation happened to run in one direction, and variation does not
persist. The next measurement drifts back toward the underlying level.

This produces a specific and damaging illusion. Intervene after the worst performance
and things improve; intervene after the best and things decline. If you attribute the
change to the intervention, you conclude that criticism works and praise does not — and
you will see that pattern consistently even if neither has any effect at all.

The correction is a comparison group, or at minimum the discipline of asking what would
have happened anyway. Without one, every intervention timed to an extreme will appear to
work.

## Military Example

A trainee shoots an unusually poor score, receives remedial coaching, and improves. The
same trainee shoots an exceptional score, is commended, and does worse next time.
Instructors across many such cycles conclude that correction is effective and praise is
counterproductive. Both observations are real and both are artefacts of when the
intervention was applied.

## Leadership Example

A department has its worst quarter, a new manager is installed, and results improve. The
improvement is credited to the manager. Some of it may be real; a substantial part would
have occurred regardless, and the same manager will look worse when they are eventually
appointed after an exceptional quarter. Judging leaders on the change from an extreme
starting point is close to judging them on when they were hired.

## Product Example

A metric drops sharply, the team responds with a fix, and the metric recovers. The fix
is credited, adopted as practice, and applied again. Unless something established what
the metric would have done without it, the team has learned nothing — and now has a
practice with a cost and no demonstrated effect, defended by a memory of it working.

## Questions

- Was this measurement taken at an extreme?
- What would have happened without the intervention?
- Do I have anything to compare against, or only a before and after?
- Am I concluding that criticism works because I only apply it after bad results?

## Common Misuse

The concept becomes a way to dismiss every genuine improvement as statistical noise,
which is as wrong as crediting everything to intervention — real effects exist and
regression does not erase them. It also applies only where variation is genuinely
present; a consistent decline with an identifiable cause is not waiting to bounce back.
And it says nothing about direction: a system can regress toward a mean that is itself
falling.
